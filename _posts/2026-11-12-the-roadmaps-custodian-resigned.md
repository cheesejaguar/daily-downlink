NVIDIA's board opened an investigation into its founder on Sunday and ended the week by accepting his resignation. The company that supplies the training compute for nearly every frontier lab now has an interim CFO as CEO, a first-ever board chair, and, per the AP, no succession plan. This week, Google put a price on a decision — $0.10 per million input tokens, output free — and this morning it shipped a mid-tier model that beats its own frontier on an independent index. The two stories share a shape: leverage is moving from the founder to the board that has to administer his roadmap, and from the model to the layer that routes it.

## A CEO exit is a supply-chain event

**What happened.** NVIDIA's board announced this morning that Jensen Huang resigned as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately. Colette Kress, executive vice president and CFO since 2013, is interim CEO while keeping the CFO role; Harvey C. Jones, former CEO and executive chairman of Synopsys, is the board's first-ever chair; and the board has begun a search for a permanent successor, internal and external. NVIDIA says Q3 fiscal 2027 results remain on schedule for November 17. The resignation closes four days that began with the board opening an independent investigation into whether Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort — on November 10 it retained former acting U.S. Attorney Joon H. Kim and Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz — and continued with a second report, late Wednesday, of an affair with an NVIDIA employee who reported to him. The AP reports NVDA fell 14% at the opening bell before stabilizing to about 4% down by midday, and that NVIDIA had no succession plan for Huang's departure. Kress told employees that "this has been an extraordinarily difficult period" and set an all-hands for November 18; Jones wrote that the board has "full confidence in Colette to deliver on our commitments to shareholders, customers, partners, and to each of you."

**Why it matters.** An operator does not buy a GPU; they buy a roadmap — a multi-year schedule of silicon, memory, software and allocation that their own capacity plan is pinned to. The CEO was the custodian of that roadmap, and the custodian left with no designated successor. The board's promise that "operations, customer commitments and product roadmap" continue without interruption is the thing to test, not to believe. The first test is November 17's guidance; the second is whether 2027 allocation and delivery terms survive a CFO-led review; the third is whether the customer commitments NVIDIA has backstopped — the neocloud buildout among them — are re-underwritten. [The 11-09 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/09/the-frontier-model-is-a-specialist-sku/) called the board probe a supply-chain event and said the tell was whether Huang's role changed at all; [the 11-10 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/10/the-model-is-a-sku-the-seat-is-the-business/) noted the docket. Today the probe became a resignation. The operator's move is contract hygiene: know your allocation terms, your escalation path, and what a leadership vacuum does to your delivery date. The stock can reprice on the headline; your capacity plan should reprice on the search.

## Google priced the decision, and it is not the cheapest

**What happened.** Google published the Gemini Decisions API yesterday: one endpoint, powered by Gemini Decision 1, that scores up to 1,000 yes/no, multiple-choice (2 to 255 options) or rubric questions against a single multimodal input of up to 256K tokens and returns probabilities and confidence scores in 150–250 milliseconds end to end. It bills $0.10 per million input tokens and zero output tokens. Google says that is nearly 5x cheaper than Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite with the Interactions API across a shared benchmark suite, and that Gemini Notebook already uses the endpoint as a real-time router, classifying intent in milliseconds for up to 40% lower latency. Artificial Analysis's Decision Index v2.1 puts Gemini Decision 1 at 66 — ninth of 18, level with Jev 1.14 at 66.23 and about seven points behind Drex Max v1.1 at 72.62 — with 211 milliseconds per task and $345 per million tasks, against $103 for Drex and $156 for Jev. On AA-Paperclip, it answers all 15 questions correctly on 33% of documents; Drex does so on 63%.

**Why it matters.** The decision-model category is where the agent's bill actually lives. Every tool call, every route, every policy check is a closed-set question, and a classifier at the boundary is cheaper than another reasoning loop — if the price per decision is lower than the loop's. Google's headline compares its token rate to a generative Flash-Lite call, not to the decision models it now competes with. Against Jev 1.14, the model it ties on score, and Drex Max v1.1, the leader, it is far more expensive per completed task: $345 per million tasks versus $156 and $103 — 2.2x and 3.3x — because Drex charges $0.03 and Jev $0.042 per million input tokens against Google's $0.10. The 1,000-questions-per-request batching is the real lever — one document read, many decisions — but accuracy per document is part of the bill. At 33% versus 63% on Paperclip, Drex needs fewer fallbacks to a human or a larger model; a cheap decision that has to be re-run is not cheap. The strategic point is the router. Google can put its own decision model inside Gemini Notebook and route each request to whichever tier is cheapest, which is [the 11-10 column's](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/10/the-model-is-a-sku-the-seat-is-the-business/) "the model is a SKU; the seat is the business" applied one layer down. When the platform owns the routing, the model's price is a variable the platform controls, not a moat. [The 11-09 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/09/the-frontier-model-is-a-specialist-sku/) called the frontier an escalation; the decision model is the opposite — the cheap workhorse every request passes through first.

## Hydrogen's 51 is real; the frontier premium is now the tail

**What happened.** Google launched Gemini 4 Hydrogen this morning at $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output, flat across its 1M-token context, in the slot where Gemini 3.8 Flash used to sit. It has four thinking levels and up to 1M output tokens, and it is available across AI Studio, the Gemini API, Gemini for business, the Gemini app, Google Search and to paid Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers in Google Antigravity. Google says Hydrogen scores 51 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its default high setting, one point above Gemini 4 Argon's 50, and 54 at xhigh; it also claims 2.5x faster task completion than 3.8 Flash at high, Terminal-Bench 4.1 at 57% versus 18.2%, and that Antigravity's new Orchestrator reaches near-Carbon success on Terminal-Bench 4.0 with 24% less quota and 19% faster. [This morning's breaking note](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/12/the-cheapest-gemini-4-outscores-its-frontier/) reported the launch on Google's numbers. Artificial Analysis has since published its own: 51 at high, ranked 21st of 78, and 54 at xhigh, ranked 16th, with 14.96 seconds to first token and 236 tokens per second at high, and 41.11 seconds and 198 tokens per second at xhigh. AA measures 6.0 minutes per Intelligence Index task and $1.87 per task at high; Carbon scores 60 at 27.1 minutes, Argon scores 50 at 30.2 minutes. AA's cost column uses Google's $4/$20 list rate for Carbon and Argon; on that basis their per-task costs are $7.76 and $9.95. Carbon's introductory $2/$10 rate runs through November 30, then steps up.

**Why it matters.** The launch claim that mattered was the one Google could not control: "our cheap model beats our expensive one" is marketing until an independent harness says it. AA's index says it — 51 versus 50 — and adds the number Google did not lead with: 6.0 minutes per index task at high against Argon's 30.2. That is the mid-tier becoming the default, and the frontier premium buying the tail rather than the average. Carbon's nine extra index points cost 4.5x the task time and, at list, about 4x the price per task. The catch is the setting. xhigh is 54 at 41 seconds to first token and $3.16 per task: "highest" is a different SKU from "default," and the operator's routing table needs both rows. The other catch is verbosity — AA counts 110 million output tokens for Hydrogen across the index at high, against 205 million for Carbon and 113 million for Argon; token price is only part of the bill. [Yesterday's column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/11/the-artifact-is-a-claim/) argued an artifact is a claim about a thing; AA's number is the evidence that Google's claim survived. [The 11-09 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/09/the-frontier-model-is-a-specialist-sku/) called the frontier an escalation; Hydrogen is the corollary — the specialist tier is now the tail of the distribution, and the default tier is where the volume goes. Google's Orchestrator makes that literal by switching models inside a session. The number to watch is not the 51; it is the first independent Terminal-Bench 4.1 run, because Google's 57% is still its own harness.

## What I'm watching

NVIDIA's November 17 guidance is the first real test of "no interruption," and the permanent-CEO search is the second; the customer commitments NVIDIA has backstopped across the neocloud market are the third. For Gemini 4 Hydrogen, the first independent Terminal-Bench 4.1 run and third-party listings matter more than the 51, and Carbon's December 1 step-up to $4/$20 puts the default-to-frontier gap at 2.7x on both input and output. For the Decisions API, the number to measure is cost per decision on your own workload, and the tell is whether Google's router sends traffic to Drex or Jev when they are cheaper — the router is the platform's pricing power. Anthropic's IPO roadshow, with trading expected around November 19, is the next market test of the same AI-trade question NVIDIA's exit opened.
