Three stories on the desk this morning point at the same shift: the model is no longer the unit of competition. DHS published the text of an order that removes DeepSeek-derived products from federal civilian IT, Meta published the reliability numbers behind its gigawatt cluster, and DeepSeek open-sourced the inference framework its own production runs on — with the operational parts left out. In each case the deciding layer sits around the model: the procurement line that admits it, the power and firmware that keep it running, and the kernels and caches that make each token cheap. That is the operator's turf, and today's news reads better as a spec sheet for it than as a model-card race.

## The procurement exclusion now reaches anything built on DeepSeek

**What happened.** DHS published the text of an order, Docket DHS-2026-0628, that excludes information technology products and services developed by DeepSeek and its affiliated entities from federal civilian agency procurements and orders the removal of "all DeepSeek and DeepSeek-affiliate products, and third-party products and services developed using DeepSeek implementations" from civilian agency information technology. The document is a notice of an order the Secretary had already issued; the news is the public text, not a new action.

**Why it matters.** The exclusion vector is lineage, not nationality. A product built on DeepSeek implementations — a fine-tune, a distillation, or a product that embeds a DeepSeek model — is caught by the removal clause even when the label on the box belongs to someone else. That turns model provenance into a compliance artifact: if you sell into federal-adjacent work, you now need to know what your vendor's vendor ran, and you need an answer that survives an audit rather than a demo. The practical shape is a two-track stack — the best open model for the lab, and a documented non-DeepSeek lineage for anything that touches a civilian agency. The notice also lands the same day [this column's own box moved to V4.1-Flash-1017](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/20/v41-flash-1017-on-two-sparks/), which is the cleanest illustration of the split: local capability and federal-adjacent sellability are now separate questions, and the gap between them is paperwork you cannot do after the fact.

## At gigawatt scale, the number that matters is goodput, not GPU count

**What happened.** Meta published "Inside Prometheus," its engineering account of the gigawatt-scale cluster in New Albany, Ohio. Phase 1 is complete: hundreds of thousands of GB200 and GB300 GPUs across multiple interconnected buildings, with Catalina (GB200 NVL72, using the two-rack Ariel configuration that links 36 GPUs per rack into a 72-GPU NVLink domain at 800G of I/O per GPU) and Clemente (GB300 NVL72) deployed, and Vera Rubin NVL72 next. Muse Wonder pre-training ran at more than 85% goodput and RL at more than 90%; wall-clock mean time between interruptions was about 47,000 GPU-hours, raised by firmware fixes to more than 54,000. The reliability target is two interruptions per 8,000 GPUs per day, or roughly 100,000 GPU-hours MTBI. The "~1 million GPUs" figure is a scaling target, not a current count.

**Why it matters.** Goodput is the fraction of the fleet doing useful work; at 85%, roughly one in seven GPU-hours is lost to stragglers, failures, and restarts. Divide the fleet-wide 54,000 GPU-hour MTBI by 200,000 GPUs and an interruption lands somewhere every 16 minutes. That is what "scale" actually buys you: not a quiet machine, but a machine whose failures are frequent enough to be routine, and whose value depends on how fast the scheduler quarantines a node and resumes the job. Meta's own numbers make the remaining work legible — the 99% reduction from peak interruption rate is real engineering, and the gap between 54,000 and the 100,000-hour target is the honest measure of what is left. You do not close that gap with another wing of accelerators; you close it with firmware, health checks, and the boring discipline of fleet operations. [The Muse models this cluster trained](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/12/meta-shipped-its-first-frontier-model-and-the-strongest-one-is-rent-only/) were this column's 10-12 story; the infrastructure behind them is the part that decides whether the next one ships on time.

## DeepSeek open-sourced the kernels and kept the service

**What happened.** DeepSeek published DeepInfer, an Apache-2.0 inference framework it describes as the one "that powers DeepSeek's large-scale production services." The release is explicitly offline inference: it supports DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and ships the optimized kernels (FlashMLA, DeepGEMM, DeepEP, TileKernels, DeepSelect), attention data parallelism and balanced context parallelism, MoE expert parallelism with EPLB, DSpark and confidence-scheduled verification, SWA bounded replay, asynchronous scheduling, batch-invariant inference, deterministic sampling, and structured outputs. It targets NVIDIA SM100 and Huawei Ascend 950. The README also lists what a complete service needs and this release does not include: a KV cache system, prefill/decode disaggregation, and load balancing across data-parallel replicas.

**Why it matters.** The open part is the part that makes each token cheap — the kernels, the collectives, the scheduler. The missing part is the part that makes a serving fleet work: the KV cache, the disaggregation, the routing. That is where the money and the tail latency live; a p50 token is cheap, and a p99 token is often a cache miss and a re-prefill. Read DeepInfer as a reference implementation of the hot path, not a turnkey server. An operator can steal the kernel patterns and the EPLB logic, but the missing pieces are the ones you would still have to build, and they are the difference between a benchmark and a service. It also confirms the pattern [this column noted on 10-12](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/12/the-inference-stack-is-unbundling/): the inference stack is unbundling, and the open lane is commoditizing the layer beneath the model while keeping the service layer proprietary. The same V4.1-Flash checkpoint that [this morning's post](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/20/v41-flash-1017-on-two-sparks/) runs at 2.9 bits is supported here, but SM100 and Ascend 950 are the targets — a desktop Spark is not on the list.

And whether Meta's goodput numbers hold as Prometheus scales toward its million-GPU target; the gap between 54,000 and 100,000 GPU-hours MTBI is the honest measure of how much engineering is left.
