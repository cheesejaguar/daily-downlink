[Yesterday's rate card](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/23/kimi-k3-1-pro-is-fourth-and-the-weights-are-a-promise/) put a million Kimi input tokens at $2. On Friday, Andy Jassy said the racks are already spoken for: AWS's 2026 capacity is sold out, 2027 is largely sold out, and a good chunk of 2028 is sold out. That is the more important price signal. When capacity is contracted years ahead, the marginal price of a token is set by allocation, not by the list — and the engineering that matters is whatever makes a fixed pool of compute go further. The day's engineering material pointed the same way: retrieval discipline that keeps the context window small, a compiler that treats latency as a search problem, and a portability table that tells you what actually runs on someone else's silicon.

## Sold out through 2027 is a price signal, not a capacity brag

**What happened.** Speaking on Andreessen Horowitz's podcast on Friday, Amazon CEO Andy Jassy said AWS's 2026 capacity is sold out, 2027 is "largely sold out" — "we always leave a certain amount for on-demand and our inference service Bedrock, or EC2, but largely sold out all the big chunks" — and "a good chunk of 2028 is sold out." Amazon plans to spend about $220 billion on capital expenditure this year and is still "leaving a meaningful amount of demand on the table," he said; the data-center, power and memory shortage, in his view, does not end in 2027. He also said inference remains "an order of magnitude too expensive" and that Trainium runs about 30% to 40% better price-performance than comparable accelerators. Amazon has not disclosed the contracted capacity or backlog behind "sold out," and neither year is fully sold out; the phrase is the CEO's characterization, not a filed number. The company reports Q3 on Thursday, October 29.

**Why it matters.** A rate card is a marginal price for a market with spare capacity. When the big blocks are pre-sold two years out, the buyer's real price is the cost of securing an allocation: commitments, reserved capacity, private offers, and the option value of being able to run on whatever silicon you can get. A $2 input rate is cheap only if the provider can serve it at the latency you need; every API price is a claim on capacity that may already be sold. The operator's move is to treat compute like a supply contract, not a utility bill — model 2027 workloads against committed capacity, not list price, and keep a portability path as the hedge. The number behind the claim is undisclosed, and much of the booked capacity sits with a handful of AI labs whose funding could tighten. Thursday's call is the test — a number on contracted capacity turns "sold out" into a fact; without one, it stays a podcast quote.

## The cheapest token is the one your agent never reads

**What happened.** LangChain published a guide to retrieval for agent context on Friday. It names three failure modes: inefficient searching that fills the window with failed queries; one search that dumps a full export or a dozen documents into the window; and stale or off-topic context that looks as convincing as the right answer, which the agent then builds on. Its Deep Agents harness searches before it reads — `glob` for names, `grep` for lines, `read_file` a page at a time — and offloads oversized tool results to files, returning only a path and a preview. For custom RAG tools, the pattern is "retrieve, offload, delegate": save results to files, return the paths, and let subagents read them.

**Why it matters.** The job of retrieval is to keep the window small, not to fill it. That inverts the usual context-engineering instinct, which treats a bigger window as the fix; the failure mode here is not too little context but too much of the wrong kind. Stale context is worse than no context because it is persuasive: once it is in the window, every subsequent step inherits the error. The architecture that follows is a filesystem with paging and delegation, not a vector dump — and it is the cheapest cost cut available, because every token in the window costs compute at prefill or decode and occupies KV cache for the rest of the loop. [This column argued in August](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/08/27/the-harness-and-the-price-of-a-task/) that the harness, not the model, sets the price of a completed task; retrieval discipline is the part of the harness that decides how many tokens the task actually costs. Measure search tokens against work tokens; if your agent opens every file to see what's inside, you have a search problem, not a context problem.

## Latency work is route search now, not kernel picking

**What happened.** NVIDIA published a two-part series on TensorRT 11.1's Global Performance Tuner on Friday. Part 1 frames an inference engine as a bundle of coupled scheduling decisions — graph-IR transformations, fusion, implementation selection, memory placement, quantization — where different choices preserve model semantics but produce very different performance. A transformation that speeds one network can slow another; a kernel that is faster in isolation can lose end to end once fusion, tensor layouts, or memory traffic interact; aggressive scheduling can improve speed while violating an accuracy requirement. Part 2 gives the design: more than 200 tunable compiler knobs, build routes as combinations of knob values, and a trtexec tuning loop that builds and measures each candidate, discards routes that crash or fail accuracy, and selects the fastest eligible engine. Accuracy filters candidates; performance ranks what remains. Across ten evaluated workloads, NVIDIA reports end-to-end gains of 1.14x to 1.63x over the default build route.

**Why it matters.** The local optimum is the trap. A kernel benchmark is not a latency number; the number that matters is the assembled engine on your workload, and the interactions only appear after the engine is built and measured. Putting accuracy inside the search rather than checking it afterward is the more interesting design decision: it treats accuracy as a constraint on the optimization, not a post-hoc veto. For an operator, the lesson transfers: if your performance work is "swap this kernel," you are optimizing one variable in a coupled system. The search is paid for in compile time — each candidate is built and measured — so tuning becomes a budget question rather than a one-shot build. [Yesterday's column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/23/the-boundary-is-the-product/) made the same point about clusters: fits is a starting condition, serves is the number. The caveat on the 1.14x–1.63x range is that it is NVIDIA's own measurement on ten workloads, with no independent replication; the mechanism is the durable part.

- **Portability is a row in a table now** — Hugging Face's torch-neuronx Space tests popular Hub models unmodified on AWS Trainium and reports the number of Trainium cores, wall time including compilation, and peak device memory, plus the exact run script, stack pins and a public NEFF cache. The caveat is the useful part: only models that fit on four Trainium2 devices are tested, the run generates 32 tokens, and a pass "says nothing about throughput, or about longer generations." A smoke test is not a performance claim; the number to ask for is cold-start wall time including compile. [huggingface.co](https://huggingface.co/blog/torch-neuronx-model-support) · [the local-box hedge](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/20/v41-flash-1017-on-two-sparks/)
- **The agent rails are neutral infrastructure now** — The Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation added four projects on October 22 — Anthropic's Agent Skills, OpenAI's Open Responses, Hugging Face's OpenEnv and Alibaba's OpenSandbox — bringing it to 10 hosted projects in its first year, up from three at launch. The layer that describes tools, skills, model interfaces and sandboxes now has a vendor-neutral home; for a team building agents, that lowers the lock-in risk of the standard layer and moves differentiation up the stack. [linuxfoundation.org](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/agentic-ai-foundation-announces-four-new-projects-agent-skills-open-responses-openenv-and-opensandbox)

The capacity buildout and the labor contraction are running in parallel; the buildout is not a hiring program. ## What I'm watching

Two dates and one number. November 4 is the only test of yesterday's rate card: if Kimi's weights land at the claimed score, the open tier gets a frontier-adjacent option at $2/$10; if they slip, the launch was a price list with a footnote. Thursday's Amazon call is the test of "sold out": a backlog number would turn a podcast quote into a disclosed fact, and a quarter of slower AWS growth would weaken the demand-exceeds-supply case. And the number I want from TensorRT is not another vendor run: it is an end-to-end tuning result on a known workload from someone who is not NVIDIA. The mechanism is already public; the third-party replication is the missing piece.
