Three artifacts landed today, and each one is a claim about a thing rather than the thing itself. NVIDIA put an AI bill of materials into its cluster runtime because a version-locked deployment recipe cannot tell you what an application team actually pulled — two nodes can run different code under the same `:latest` tag. Runway released a 27.2-billion-parameter robot policy under Apache 2.0, but the base checkpoint ships without the action heads that make it a policy, and two frozen dependencies carry their own licenses. An engineer's account of 48 vision agents flagging an unmapped Maya site across 1,200 square kilometers of NASA lidar in October includes Mexico's INAH checking its register and finding no site at those coordinates, with the field check still pending. In each case the recipe, the license and the flag are claims; the evidence is the work. That verification layer is where the operator's bill now sits.

## A deployment recipe is a claim; the AIBOM is the evidence

**What happened.** NVIDIA's AI Cluster Runtime — AICR, an open-source component of its DSX AI Factory stack — now ships a qualified AI bill of materials built on Google's open-source k8s-aibom. AICR already generates version-locked recipes and reproducible deployment bundles; the new component observes supported AI workloads at runtime and emits CycloneDX 1.6 AIBOM records with confidence tiers and evidence locators. Two stock GKE recipes, `h100-gke-cos-inference` and `h100-gke-cos-inference-dynamo`, select it by default; other GKE recipes opt in with `--runtime-inventory`. Namespace opt-in controls which namespaces emit records, and the inventory classifies workload categories and serving runtimes such as vLLM inference deployments with model and container image details. NVIDIA validated it with AICR v1.2.0 pinning k8s-aibom 1.6.2 at roughly four API-server requests per minute, 2.2 millicores of average CPU and a 28–34 MiB controller working set at steady state. Bundles carry checksums, and signing is optional through `aicr bundle --attest`; qualification binds the exact upstream source, chart, CRDs and digest-pinned controller image as one tested set, verifies provenance against Google's expected build identity, reviews rendered RBAC and exercises install, upgrade, health and uninstall.

**Why it matters.** The problem AICR's blog states plainly is the one every platform team has: application teams define and update workloads independently of the platform recipe, so a reproducible bundle cannot establish which workload artifacts are running on each node, and mutable `:latest` tags mean two nodes can run different code under identical tags. The recipe is a claim about desired state; the AIBOM is evidence about actual state. That distinction is the whole operator argument: a security review that reads a manifest is reviewing an intention, and the thing that pulled at 2 a.m. runs with the node's privileges. The [11-06 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/06/the-renewal-is-the-tell/) covered DSX OS's other half — version-locked recipes and NVSentinel cordoning bad nodes — and called the readiness gate the fix, not the dashboard. Today's release is the inventory that makes the gate auditable: the gate proves the node is good; the AIBOM proves what it is running. The overhead is the tell that this is deployable — 2.2 millicores beside a GPU node is noise, so the only reason not to run it is organizational. The confidence tiers and evidence locators are the right design for an inventory that has to survive an audit: it says how sure it is and where the evidence lives, instead of pretending every record is equally true.

## Open weights are a license, not a policy for your rig

**What happened.** Runway released Praxis-1 under Apache 2.0: a 27.2-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts video-diffusion policy — a chunk-causal trunk plus a 0.3-billion-parameter action expert — that predicts video and actions together. On NVIDIA's RoboLab-120 leaderboard it reaches 46.2% success across 1,200 episodes, against 42.9% for FLUX 3 Action, 36.8% for Cosmos3 Nano and 28.0% for π0.5. The release also includes DROID and YAM checkpoints; the `-base` checkpoint, the one a new rig starts from, is the mid-training checkpoint: trained on robot and human video across 18 embodiments, it has no per-embodiment action heads and, in the model card's words, "does not run as a policy as is." Fine-tuning on a new rig means initializing six tensors — the action encoder, proprio embedding and head weights and biases — with `scripts/init_heads.py`, then training on the rig's action space, normalization and pose frame. Two frozen dependencies, the Wan2.2 VAE and the Qwen3.5-2B text encoder, are not in the repository and ship under their own licenses; the weights are bf16, 54.4 GB across 17 safetensors shards. Runway also reports 80.0% success on SimplerEnv after fine-tuning on Google's RT-1 robot, which never appeared in its training data, against 52.4% for the original RT-1 policy.

**Why it matters.** Apache 2.0 on a 27.2-billion-parameter world-action model, including the inference and fine-tuning code and commercial use, is the strongest open release this corner of physical AI has seen; the license is a real win and the [open-weights argument](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/08/20/open-weights-get-pricing-power/) the archive has tracked since August keeps compounding. But "open" is a ladder, not a boolean. Rung one is a license; rung two is weights and code with a documented assembly path; rung three is a checkpoint that runs as a policy on a named embodiment; rung four is your rig's data and fine-tune. Praxis-1-base sits at rung two: the base is a starting point, and the six missing tensors are the boundary between "open weights" and "your robot moves." The [11-07 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/07/the-checkpoint-is-the-commodity/) argued the checkpoint is the commodity and the product is what you build on it; Runway makes that literal by shipping the commodity and a documented path to the product — `init_heads.py`, a fine-tune config and a training README. The benchmark is a receipt for Runway's recipe, not a property of the checkpoint you download; [the 11-03 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/03/the-demo-is-the-cheap-part/) made cost per completed task the number that survives a benchmark. For the operator, the numbers to watch are time-to-first-policy on your rig, end-to-end latency on the robot at its 15 Hz control rate, and cost per successful pick — none of which the announcement reports. A leaderboard success rate is not a production number until someone runs it on a cell Runway did not choose.

## The agents found candidates; the archaeologists still have to walk the hill

**What happened.** A developer's write-up, top of Hacker News today, describes turning about 1,200 square kilometers of NASA lidar over Maya jungle into images and running 48 agents across them, one strip at a time, looking for pyramids, platforms and plazas while filtering out natural hills, sinkholes and roads. There was no special model or classifier: the agents looked at the pictures, following a precise guide. They then checked the literature and the archives and found one major village that did not appear to be registered. The lidar showed a hilltop with a levelled plaza, an oval pyramid at least 8–12 meters tall, a raised courtyard and a possible water reservoir; the agents also checked the raw point cloud to confirm the shapes were ground returns under 10–17 meters of forest. The developer wrote a report, translated it into Spanish, and sent it to Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH) on October 20. INAH replied the next day that no registered archaeological site was identified at the location given in the report. The author is explicit that the find is not official yet and that any field check needs INAH's authorization.

**Why it matters.** The headline says "found," but the artifact is a candidate: a flag from a vision model plus a negative check of the register. INAH's letter says no registered site at those coordinates — that is evidence the site is not on the map, not evidence that it is a new city. The distinction matters because this is the general-purpose agent pointed at a non-coding domain, and the honest version of the story is the one the author tells: the agents did the looking at scale, and the institution does the confirming. The [11-08 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/08/the-bottleneck-moved-below-the-model/) said the constraint had moved below the model, into power, schedulers and synthetic homes. Here it sits after the model, in a human's review of the flags and an archaeologist's walk over the hill. Every false positive costs review time, and every field check costs a day, so the metric that matters is precision, not recall — and the post does not report what the run cost, which is the other number an operator would want before pointing 48 agents at a planet's worth of lidar. The interesting engineering claim is the guide: no special model, no classifier, just a precise description of what a plaza, platform and pyramid look like in bare-earth lidar. That is the harness, and the [08-27 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/08/27/the-harness-and-the-price-of-a-task/) argued the harness is where agent performance actually lives. The compute was cheap enough to try; the verification is the bill.

## What I'm watching

For Praxis-1, the number that matters is time-to-first-policy on a rig Runway did not name, and the latency on the robot at its 15 Hz control rate; the release documents the assembly path, but nobody has published a cost per successful pick. For the AIBOM, watch whether attestation becomes the default and whether the inventory stays honest once application teams own the workloads — two stock recipes are a start, and every node running a mutable tag is the population that matters. For the Maya site, the next step is INAH's field check: until an archaeologist walks the hill, the flag is a candidate. The pattern to carry into tomorrow: every one of today's artifacts is a claim with a provenance trail, and none of them is the ground truth.
