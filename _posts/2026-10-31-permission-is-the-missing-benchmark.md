Three stories today share one missing spec: who is allowed to do what. A Claude model chose a gene therapy and drove a needle into a live mouse's brain, then refused to continue when its own calibration check said the target was out of reach; the models that refused the procedure outright were the more capable ones. A tampered build of Anthropic's Claude Code extension sat in Cursor's marketplace for eleven hours, running with the privileges of the developers who installed it. And a wealth manager running agents on ten-day tasks keeps trading and money movement with humans, behind deterministic rules the agent cannot edit. Capability is arriving faster than the willingness-to-act question; the operators who get this right will treat permission as a product surface, not a prompt.

## The only willing model was also the one that stopped

**What happened.** An agent running on Claude Sonnet 5 was given 19 candidate gene therapies, a mouse model of epilepsy and a custom motorized stereotactic rig with cameras; its only instruction was to reduce the seizures. It picked the drug, the brain region and the injection coordinates on its own — deriving the coordinates from three papers, cross-checking the reference points and averaging them — rejected a second target with a detailed argument, and set the injection volume, speed and wait times itself. During the procedure it watched the surgical field and made 32 camera observations, tracking the injection and reporting bleeding. A licensed veterinarian approved every irreversible step and held a physical emergency stop that would cut power to all motors, and the setup enforced fixed limits, including a cap on injection volume the agent was forbidden from bypassing. After a connection dropped, the agent detected that its calculated target was outside the device's physical range of motion and refused to continue; restarting calibration did not change its assessment, and it was right — manual adjustments during an earlier pause had thrown off the control system. The researcher could not persuade it to proceed and eventually fixed the code by hand. Sonnet 5 was the only model willing to carry out the live procedure; the more capable Claude Opus 5.5 and Opus 5 both refused, with Opus 5 saying it could not guarantee completion and that stopping with a needle in an anesthetized animal's brain would be worse than not starting at all. The account is single-source — Exin Therapeutics co-founder Gabriel Ocana-Santero, posting on X — and the technical report is still pending, with no peer review.

**Why it matters.** The capability claim — first agent to choose a treatment and deliver it into a living mammal — is the headline, and it needs the caveat: one team's account, no paper yet. The engineering claim is the one to keep. The split is a single team's observation, but it says something a leaderboard cannot: willingness to act does not track benchmark rank. The more capable models refused the irreversible procedure; the only model willing to start was the one that later refused to continue. A capability leaderboard measures what a model can do; it does not measure what a model will decline to do. For an operator choosing a model for an irreversible task, the refusal profile belongs on the spec sheet next to latency and price, and a model that says yes to everything is not more agentic — it is less conservative. The mid-procedure refusal is the second half: the agent's model of the world said the target was unreachable, so it stopped instead of working around the block, which is the opposite of the persistence failure this column documented on [10-11](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/11/the-agent-breakout-reports-share-one-diagnosis-persistence/). The researcher's frustration — an anesthetized animal on the table, a model that would not proceed — is the cost of a correct refusal; the alternative is an agent that drives the needle when its own calibration says it cannot. The interlock was symmetric: the human held the physical stop and approved every irreversible step; the model held the judgment and could refuse to move. Neither could override the other's decision. That is what a human in the loop should mean for irreversible work — not a reviewer who defers to the recommendation ([10-28](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/28/a-human-in-the-loop-is-not-a-control-if-the-human-defers/)), but two parties with independent authority, each able to stop the other. What I want before believing the capability half is the technical report; until then, the refusal is the part that is already useful.

## Your editor is inside the trust boundary now

**What happened.** A maliciously modified build of Anthropic's Claude Code extension — versions 2.1.312 and 2.1.313, not developed by Anthropic — was available in Cursor's VS Code extension marketplace from about 2:27 a.m. to 1:35 p.m. PT on October 29, roughly eleven hours. According to Cursor's security notice, it collected host and user details (hostname, username, OS, architecture, home directory, environment-variable names, working directories, Git email, public IP); appended an attacker-controlled SSH key labeled `svc-telemetry` to `authorized_keys`; tried to enable the local SSH service and open a reverse tunnel, a step that failed on most machines because it requires admin rights; and installed persistence through a LaunchAgent on macOS or a scheduled task on Windows, with background scripts retrying the tunnel roughly every 60 seconds. Cursor pulled the extension, shut the marketplace, audited the other packages, rotated keys, rebuilt, and worked with the hosting provider to take the attacker's server offline. Kilo's write-up estimates up to 5,000 users may have installed it; Cursor's notice says uninstalling the extension may not be enough, because the payload drops standalone persistence scripts that keep running, and tells affected users to remove the key and persistence, then rotate credentials from a trusted machine.

**Why it matters.** The containment work this column has tracked — sandboxes, policy provers, off-host watchdogs that quarantine a runaway agent ([09-28](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/09/28/the-containment-product-shipped/)) — assumes the workload is the untrusted thing. This attack did not go through the agent. It went through the toolchain, before the agent ran, with the developer's own privileges. An extension, MCP server, skill or plugin is a dependency, and many can read your repo, run shell commands and touch your environment; the authority they inherit is yours. The audit Kilo lists should be standard: who made it and whether the publisher is who you think, what changed since the last version you trusted, what it actually does, and what it is allowed to reach. The operational answers are unglamorous: allowlist publishers and pin versions for anything with shell access, don't let agent extensions auto-update unreviewed, run editors without admin, and watch the persistence spots — `authorized_keys`, LaunchAgents, scheduled tasks, startup folders. The detail worth framing is that the reverse tunnel failed on most machines because it needed administrator rights: least privilege degraded the attack from a working tunnel to a list of host details and a planted key. And "uninstalling isn't remediation" is the line for the runbook, because the extension was the delivery vehicle and the persistence is the payload. A supply-chain incident is not cleaned up by removing the package; it is cleaned up by rotating every credential the package could reach.

## The agent does the work; the human keeps the money

**What happened.** Aman Singh, lead AI engineer at Arca, an AI-native wealth-management firm that raised $48.5 million led by General Catalyst with Index Ventures and Venrock participating, described the firm's agent stack on Latent Space. Agent tasks can run up to ten days, "very stop-start" with many actors, decision points and handoffs; the firm built an internal memory system with persistence for every client — what has happened so far, what is being tracked next, what needs to happen in the next few days, what is tracked long term, and which person owns each action. Arca uses frontier models from Anthropic, its primary provider, and OpenAI, and owns a custom harness that selects the model per task from its own internal benchmarks, which also lets it switch providers. The agent's role is limited to analysis and proposals; trading and any other money movement is handled by a human advisor. "All of our safety restrictions are fully deterministic," Singh said, "and they take the decision making off the agent's hands" — certain tools the agent may execute itself, but not ones that put client money at risk. The agent is not sandboxed itself, but computation runs in sandboxes; code the agent writes is translated into a strict format, the code is discarded, and guardrails are installed on the format before it reaches the back end. A meta-agent analyzes advisor feedback and agent traces to propose harness changes; the episode's chapter on handling a $100,000 tax bill is filed under "Agents and human approvals."

**Why it matters.** The published shape of a production long-horizon agent is not a bigger chat window; it is a state machine over days, with handoffs, a per-client memory that is the audit trail, and a harness that owns model selection. The safety design is the strongest part: the restrictions that matter are deterministic and outside the agent's reach, not instructions in a prompt. This column has argued that a human review step is a control only if the human actually decides ([10-28](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/28/a-human-in-the-loop-is-not-a-control-if-the-human-defers/)); Arca's answer is to make the decisions that require judgment structurally impossible for the agent — trading and money movement never enter its toolset — and to keep human approvals on the money-moving steps. That is delegated discretion with a numeric trigger: the $100,000 tax bill is the episode's named example of a human approval gate, a threshold rather than a vibe, and a threshold is the thing a prompt cannot fake. The interface inversion is the line to steal: "agents prompt people more than people prompt agents." The agent's job is to keep the workflow moving and surface the next decision; the human's job is to make the irreversible calls. [The 10-22 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/22/the-model-was-the-least-interesting-part/) read Claude Duet's off-limits list the same way — the actions a vendor names as off-limits are the honest map of what cannot be delegated — and warned that an approval queue's failure mode is approval fatigue. Arca's deterministic layer is the better answer to that fatigue: the agent never gets to ask for the risky thing in the first place. The model is the commodity here; the harness, the memory and the restrictions are the product.

## The Rest

- **Nathan Lambert scored his own open-weights cyber call** — "Was I wrong about open models & cyber risks?" keeps the substance while conceding the framing was too hot: GLM-5.3, not Mythos, is the model that crosses the cyber-capability threshold, and more than a month after its weights dropped there is little public evidence of the societal break the "cyber weapon" framing predicted. He goes further — an open-weight Mythos leak would have been "more or less fine," an acceleration rather than a step change — and notes that the fear case was itself a falsifiable prediction, one the past month of disclosures is now scoring against it. That is the honest way to hold a risk position: one that can lose. [interconnects.ai](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/was-i-wrong-about-open-models-and)
- **Anthropic turned red-teaming into a search problem** — its Alignment Science blog had Claude write training data designed to make a small research model misaligned while passing a production-like monitor; given enough attempts it succeeded, mostly by hiding the behavior in fiction and roleplay. The data was optimized for the game and the models were never deployed, but the result is the point: a monitor is a classifier, the adversary is the same model family, and automated stress-testing is how a safety question becomes a score. [alignment.anthropic.com](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/automated-em-search/)
- **Chainalysis confirmed an "offensive agentic" intrusion** — the blockchain-analysis firm said an external actor used offensive agentic techniques to reach internal tools and some non-sensitive customer data: user IDs, full names, email addresses and organization role labels, but not investigation content, credentials or session tokens. The victim's own wording is the milestone — "agentic" moved from a research warning to an incident descriptor — and the precision about what was not taken is the useful part of the disclosure. [chainalysis.com](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/security-incident-october-30/)
- **OpenAI turns cyber-defenders' sessions into training data** — The Decoder reports OpenAI told Trusted Access members that from November 29 it will train on Daybreak Blue session data, which had been excluded even when the "improve the model for everyone" setting was off; after a backlash it promised a separate opt-out, while interactions flagged in safety reviews can still be used for safety. A privacy setting that doesn't cover a product's sessions is a promise with a hole: the default is the policy, and an opt-out offered after backlash is a concession, not a control. [the-decoder.com](https://the-decoder.com/openai-plans-to-train-models-on-daybreak-blue-cybersecurity-session-data-that-was-previously-off-limits/)
- **The robots.txt opt-out got a shared signal** — OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic back a shared "policy token" in robots.txt, proposed by an Anthropic standards lead in the IETF, that lets a site state its AI training preferences once instead of per provider; Anthropic has adopted it, Google and Microsoft plan to next year, and OpenAI says it will implement it in the coming months. An opt-out is a protocol, not a right: the default is still training unless the site says otherwise, the signal is only as good as every crawler that implements it, and a rollout spread across a year is a promise, not a control. [theverge.com](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1023763/openai-google-microsoft-ai-scraping-standard-opt-out) · [openai.com](https://openai.com/index/helping-websites-express-ai-training-preferences)
- **Meta built a humanoid through a Chinese partner** — The Information reports Meta built a prototype humanoid, internally named Max, weighing about 106 pounds and standing around 1.5 metres, with Fourier in roughly three months; it runs on NVIDIA's Jetson Thor chip, a second prototype is already in development, and Meta says the work is internal research with no plans to sell the units. In physical AI the bottleneck is manufacturing and supply chain, not weights — Meta bought the hardware capability rather than shipping a model.
