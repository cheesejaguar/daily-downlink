The day's three useful stories are all about who signs the check, and none of the signatures came from the thing being checked. NVIDIA's board reinstated Jensen Huang after receiving input from senior levels of the U.S. government, reversing a resignation it had accepted earlier this month and attaching a national-security committee and a compliance-officer search to the decision. Google DeepMind's agent teams found faster quantum circuits — an 11.8× cost cut on a fusion simulation — but the results only count because a Lean kernel checked them. And a Lawrence Livermore benchmark found that when the simulator is secretly wrong, most models report a wrong number while claiming 95% confidence. For an operator, the value of an AI result is the independence of its check.

## The government co-signed the CEO's return

**What happened.** NVIDIA's board voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately, undoing the resignation it had accepted. The board said it was satisfied with Huang's "candor and ability to lead NVIDIA" after a series of discussions, and that it had "received input from senior levels of the United States government" and is confident the reinstatement "poses no national security risk." Harvey Jones remains board chair, Colette Kress returns to her CFO duties, and the independent-director committee investigating the foreign-intelligence reports will continue to its conclusion with independent counsel. The board also formed a National Security and Export Control Committee and began a search for a Chief Compliance Officer. NVDA surged 33% on the news, and Senate Banking Chairman Tim Scott said Huang will testify publicly on December 3, as the sole witness at a hearing titled "Protecting America's AI Advantage: Oversight of U.S. Export Controls."

**Why it matters.** The reversal removes the CEO-search discount from NVIDIA's roadmap, and that matters to anyone whose capacity plan is pinned to it — the custodian is back, after [the 11-12 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/12/the-roadmaps-custodian-resigned/) called the exit a supply-chain event. But the check that cleared Huang was external and political: the board's confidence rests on input from the executive branch, not on the completion of its own investigation, which is still running; Bloomberg reported the decision was made without consulting NVIDIA's senior leadership, who were informed at the last minute. That makes the government a standing input to NVIDIA's governance, and it converts the export-control question from a founder relationship into a compliance function. The tell is not the stock pop; it is whether the new Chief Compliance Officer has authority over a shipment before it leaves, and what the December 3 hearing puts on the record. [The 11-15 zeitgeist](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/15/sunday-zeitgeist-the-week-the-control-plane-became-the-product/) predicted the board would name a permanent CEO from outside the company by December 31; it returned the role to the founder instead. That call is wrong, and it stays on the record.

## Proof-checked search is the product; the qubits are still the bill

**What happened.** Google DeepMind's post today describes a paper, first posted to arXiv on November 6, in which independent teams of Gemini 4 agents synthesized quantum circuits against formal specifications in Lean, a theorem prover. The teams cut the computing cost of an inertial-confinement-fusion simulation by 11.8× and lowered its memory requirement from 5,650 to 4,338 error-corrected qubits; they made a photochemistry simulation 8× cheaper and improved a Cytochrome P450 simulation 2.5× (481 to 333 qubits); they found a 2.57× speedup for a specific Grover search implementation and a 290× speedup for quantum arcsine. Independent teams traded ideas at a virtual conference every four hours, and only Lean-checked results entered the shared notebook. DeepMind open-sourced the Lean library, the problem specifications and the verified solutions on GitHub. Its own post says the circuits would still demand more resources than the first error-corrected quantum computers are likely to provide.

**Why it matters.** The 11.8× is a compression of the design search, not a claim that the application now fits on near-term hardware — 4,338 error-corrected qubits is still a hardware program with a date on it, and if the hardware slips, the algorithmic win waits. The durable artifact is the loop: independent attempts, a deterministic filter, and a shared notebook that only accepts checked results. That is the template agent engineering has been missing. Lean proves the circuit meets the stated correctness and cost specification; it does not prove the problem is the right one, or that the cost model matches the machine. The spec stays human-owned, and problem selection — not idea generation — becomes the bottleneck. [The 11-21 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/21/stop-the-clock-after-the-check/) made the point about checking the statement, not just the proof; this is the same division of labor, run as a research loop instead of a one-off.

## A benchmark that grades calibration is a deployment gate

**What happened.** A Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory paper first posted November 16 introduces SciResearch-Eval, an interactive benchmark built on a standard materials-science task: measure a material's thermal properties by heating it and fitting a heat-conduction simulator. The agent designs its own experiments; hidden from it, the simulator may be under-resolved, physically wrong, or fed uninformative data; and it must report conductivity, heat capacity and a heat-transfer coefficient with 95% intervals against exact ground truth. On a held-out split of 93 tasks, nine models and 2,511 episodes, most models report wrong values while stating they are 95% sure — their intervals contain the truth in 20–62% of cases, against 94% for GPT-6 Astra and 99% for Claude Fable 5.2. At default effort, the failing Claude 5.5 models diagnose the missing physics in only 35% of episodes (Astra: 66%); at maximum effort Opus improves more than tenfold but uses 13–31× more reasoning. Asking for wider intervals does not help; telling the model to state its strategy improves interval scores 1.6–2.5×, but coverage barely moves. Fable 5.2 is the most accurate at default — an interval score 1.9× better than Astra's — but writes six times as many tokens, and at Astra's token count it falls short. The environment, graders and harness are released.

**Why it matters.** The production failure here is not a wrong point estimate; it is an interval the agent has no right to. If the model says 95% and the truth falls outside its interval 38–80% of the time, every downstream automation that trusts the interval inherits a false confidence rate — which is worse than an agent that says it does not know, because the downstream system cannot tell the difference. The detail that asking for wider intervals does not help is the useful one: calibration is not humility theater. Astra stays calibrated by designing gentle-heating experiments where the flawed simulator is accurate rather than by reporting wider error bars — it probes the instrument's validity, then chooses experiments that avoid the broken region. That is the operator move: test the instrument, not just the model. And the effort ablation is the cost line: the capability is latent in Opus 5.5, but a tenfold interval-score improvement for 13–31× more reasoning is a budget decision, not a free switch. It is also the shape [the 11-22 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/22/the-numbers-landed-the-artifacts-didnt/) asked for: a result that ships the harness and the ground truth beside the score.

## The Rest

- **Z.ai's GLM-5.4 runs the same 28 layers twice** — the 750B flagship is built on a Loop Transformer: 28 layers of the GLM-5 base execute two iterations per forward pass, with a 1M context and vision. Z.ai says an extra loop costs one sliding window of KV cache rather than a full context cache, and output length on GPQA-Diamond and MMLU-Pro drops 10–14% because part of the reasoning moves into the loop; the post points to its Coding Plan and ZCode, not to downloadable weights. The operator question is whether the depth shows up as throughput or as latency on the bill. [z.ai](https://z.ai/blog/glm-5.4)
- **Hugging Face's agentic-evals guide starts with the claim, not the dashboard** — claim-first design, which components to reuse versus adapt, contamination versus saturation, and a strategic open-sourcing checklist; its appendix walks an agentic deployment in a physics lab through eight steps from claim to score. The reusable part is the order: state the claim, pin the harness, then measure. [huggingface.co](https://huggingface.co/blog/agentic-evals)
- **Kilo's cost metric counts the changes that survive review** — cost per accepted change divides total inference spend by accepted patches, including abandoned attempts and human review minutes; its arithmetic example has a $1.20 run with four accepted changes at $0.30 each losing to a $2.40 run with twelve accepted at $0.20. The post says the figures illustrate the arithmetic, not benchmark results. [blog.kilo.ai](https://blog.kilo.ai/p/what-does-an-accepted-ai-code-change)
- **AscendGYM benchmarks the agent, not the chip** — the Shenzhen Loop Area Institute released a cost-aware kernel-optimization benchmark for Huawei Ascend NPUs, scoring cross-case applicability, shape specialization and optimization cost; Opus 5 leads, GPT-5.6 is more token-efficient, GLM-5.3 is close behind, and Claude Code gives the best absolute scores. A kernel benchmark is how a chip gap gets closed in software — and the cost dimension is what makes it an operator benchmark instead of a leaderboard. [github.com](https://github.com/SLAI-AITP/AscendGYM) · [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17621)
- **The private market marked the layer between the model and the bill** — earlier today we noted Sierra's $1.75 billion raise at a $30 billion valuation, about 75 times its $400 million ARR, and Amazon's agreement to buy Decart's optimization stack for about $7.5 billion. Two marks on the same thesis: the margin is in the layer that makes the model usable, not in the model. ## What I'm watching

The December 3 Senate hearing is the next dated test of the NVIDIA story: whether a public export-control oversight hearing produces a written rule or just a rhetorical one, and whether the new Chief Compliance Officer has authority before the next China shipment. The DeepMind result is checkable — the Lean library is public — so the tell is whether a second lab extends a spec or reproduces a circuit, not whether the blog post gets cited. And SciResearch-Eval's harness is released, so the calibration gap will either replicate on open-weight models with more tooling or turn out to be a frontier-lab advantage; either answer changes how much autonomy you can hand an agent that touches an instrument.
