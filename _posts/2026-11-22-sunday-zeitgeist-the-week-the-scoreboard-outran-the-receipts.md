The scoreboard got loud this week and the receipts got thin. Anthropic's first trade put a $2.01 trillion market cap on the board, NVIDIA guided its next quarter to $130 billion, and OpenAI cut its frontier rate card by 20% — real numbers, every one of them arithmetic on something. Then you read the footnotes: the market cap is a share count at a demand price, the NVIDIA guide gives back three points of gross margin to memory costs, and OpenAI's 20% cut arrives with a 60% discount on cached input aimed at exactly the workload agents produce. The three most consequential technical claims of the week — a perplexity metric proposed as the basis for common safety limits, a practical cryptanalysis of NIST candidates, and a 3,554-flaw patch wave — each arrived without the artifact that would let an outsider check it. The one claim that was certified, a 17-million-line Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, was certified in a day because the smallest file in the chain pinned the statement. Agentic AI's bottleneck has moved from generation to certification: the number is now free, and the check is the product.

## The scoreboard got its number; the receipt didn't ship

Anthropic priced at $83, opened Thursday at $111.50 for a market cap of about $2.01 trillion, closed at $97.17, and traded at $84.77 by Friday afternoon — three prices for the same company in two days, and [the 11-19 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/19/the-scoreboard-got-its-number-the-plumbing-got-the-bill/) called the first print a demand read rather than a re-rating. The raise closed at $119.5 billion gross, and $26 billion of it was a private placement to AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, NVIDIA and TSMC at the offer price: the companies that sell Anthropic its accelerators, memory, foundry and cloud capacity now own its equity, against a $900.9 billion commitment book that is largely owed back to companies like them. NVIDIA's first print under interim CEO Colette Kress beat consensus at $115.8 billion in revenue, up 103%, and guided Q4 to $130.0 billion, with gross margin stepping from 74.9% to 71–72% as memory costs land. OpenAI shipped the successor to a cancelled flagship at 20% below the old card, and Google launched Gemini 4 Neon at $3/$15 two weeks before Carbon and Argon step from $2/$10 to $4/$20. The price ladder that inverted [last week](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/15/sunday-zeitgeist-the-week-the-control-plane-became-the-product/) inverted again: the frontier keeps advancing while its rate card deflates.

What did not ship was the check. OpenAI's chief scientist published a perplexity curve and proposed common safety limits on the metric — measured, by the post's own footnote, on OpenAI's internal codebase, with no shared evaluation corpus, tokenizer or harness. A limit on that number would be a private number in public clothing: every lab reporting its own unverifiable figure, nobody able to reproduce the comparison. MiniMax reported practical breaks on historical NIST candidates — a Waterfall-256 collision in 0.045 seconds, ROLLO-I-128 key recovery in about six seconds — and published the proof-of-concept code, but not the model; no outside cryptographer has run the attacks, so what is reproducible is the script, not the capability that wrote it. Microsoft patched 3,554 security flaws in the ten weeks before its November 12 outage, while its postmortem attributes the outage to a July bug its lab hardware could not reproduce: 3,554 is a discovery metric, the outage is a change-management and test-fidelity metric, and only one of them tells you what to fix. Tesla's FSD v15 release notes claim a 25x improvement in the critical intervention rate with no published baseline or methodology. [Today's column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/22/the-numbers-landed-the-artifacts-didnt/) put the pattern plainly: three numbers landed, each without the artifact that would let someone else check it.

For a builder, the practical consequence is a change in the procurement question. It used to be "what's your score?" Now it is "show me the artifact" — the corpus, the tokenizer, the harness version, the seeds, the held-out split, the reviewer's hit rate. A benchmark number without those is a receipt for a recipe you cannot inspect, which is the distinction [the 11-20 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/20/the-machinery-around-the-model-got-its-numbers/) drew from a harness-trained LoRA and [the 11-17 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/17/the-cancelled-release-came-back-cheaper/) drew from a 98%-at-40% claim whose 40% was a promotional price. What gets commoditized is the score itself: anyone can publish a chart. What gets scarce is the reproducible harness and the independent check. The assumption retired is that a headline number is evidence of a capability. The lab that publishes the yardstick first owns the standard — and it probably will not be a lab, because a lab grading its own private corpus is grading its own homework.

## The check is the product, and the market started paying for it

The week's one certified artifact is the template. Levent Alpöge released a 17-million-line Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis on November 20; the Lean and Mathlib communities certified it about a day later. The load-bearing step was the smallest file in the chain: a file that isolates the statement and imports only Mathlib, which two independent tools — comparator and `con-leche` — confirmed proves exactly Mathlib's `RiemannHypothesis`. The kernel then checked the exported proof, alongside checkers that carry formal proofs they cannot accept a false proof, on an inert export out of reach of hypothetical malicious code in the proof sources. [The 11-21 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/21/stop-the-clock-after-the-check/) made the point: a compile proves the proof compiles, not that it proves the theorem in the headline. Two residuals stay open — the Lean post certifies "Riemann" while Tao's roundup calls the same artifact the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and the kernel says nothing about whether the strategy is a proof a number theorist recognizes. But the template is portable, and it is the same division of labor [the 09-05 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/09/05/claude-formalized-fermats-last-theorem-in-lean/) described for Fermat's Last Theorem: pin the spec, compare it independently, and run the proof through checkers the proof cannot influence.

That template is showing up in agent engineering, minus the kernel. Kilo published a protocol for timing parallel agents that starts the clock before the first worktree and stops it after integration and final review — its own example has three agents finishing in ten minutes and the merge eating twenty more. It contains no measured results, which is the most honest thing about it: the contribution is the denominator. Parallel-agent claims usually stop the clock when the agents return, excluding exactly the work that scales with agent count — merge, repair and review. OpenAI's Performance Factory is the production version: more than 1,700 regression investigations in three months, more than 600 root-caused, 17 a day, and a manual audit of 100 root-cause suggestions graded 70% accurate. That is roughly four defensible diagnoses a day — a reviewer's queue of a few hours, not a solved problem. The adversarial Codex session that reviews each diagnosis is a monitor, not an anchor: it is another Codex session checking a Codex diagnosis, and OpenAI publishes no catch rate for it. The only independent number is the human-graded audit, which says 30% are wrong.

LangChain's knowledge factory is the same shape applied to context. Agents flag what they cannot find; a curator agent runs on a cadence with access to raw sources, traces and flags, and rewrites the corpus. The post names no review step for the curator's diffs, and the curator is a standing writer with credentials to every source — which makes the diff, not the document, the reviewable unit, and makes the storage choice matter more than the post allows, because only some stores give you a diff history. Hugging Face's harness-adapter result is the training-side version: a LoRA trained inside Pi, OpenCode and Hermes moved a frozen Qwen3.8-27B from 39.2% to 64.7% on the 51 hardest SWE-bench Pro tasks, with 56–72% fewer tool calls on the tasks both they and the base solve. But the authors say plainly that the number is a receipt for a triple of model, harness and eval config, that cross-harness comparison is invalid, and that on 300 AutomationBench tasks run with a different harness the adapters stayed close to base. The gain is a specialist's, not a general capability, and the operator rule follows: a harness version belongs in the eval pin, and a harness upgrade is a model change.

Read together, the production number for an agent is not tasks generated and not tokens served. It is completed-and-checked units per attempt, with the review cost and latency beside it. Generation got cheap; the queue is the bill — [the 11-14 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/14/generation-got-cheap-review-did-not/) said it first, and [last week](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/15/sunday-zeitgeist-the-week-the-control-plane-became-the-product/) the capital followed the layer: LangChain, Mercor and Bay raised for traces, evals and pre-execution checks. This week the layer shipped its production numbers, and Alibaba's September quarter made the same point from the P&L: AI cloud up 53% with an RMB8.84 billion adjusted profit, while AI labs and applications — the segment that houses Qwen — posted an RMB10.50 billion adjusted loss. The cloud is profitable; the model is a cost center. What gets commoditized is the model and the token. What gets scarce is the falsifier, the reviewer's time, and the pinned spec. The assumption retired is that an agent's own claim of completion is a completion.

## The constraint moved to the permit desk and the license queue

The buildout's other clock ran through government desks. BIS released an interim final rule on Monday that requires a license to export, reexport or transfer advanced super-intelligence items when the exporter knows they are destined for a data center anywhere in the world providing more than 12 million aggregate TPP to companies headquartered in Macau or Country Group D:5 — and it counts "linked data centers," so a cluster split across sites is still one footprint. In the same document it rescinded the AI Diffusion Rule and removed the model-weight control. The tell is what the government gave up: weights are not the chokepoint; the ability to train is. By Thursday the rule's unit had changed in practice — TSMC and Samsung suspended some shipments to Chinese customers at 16-nanometer processes and below while they apply for U.S. licenses, effectively limiting affected designers to 28- and 40-nanometer processes without high-bandwidth memory or advanced packaging. [The 11-19 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/19/the-scoreboard-got-its-number-the-plumbing-got-the-bill/) put it exactly: the control used to be a technical judgment a foundry could make from a design's characteristics; now it is a customer file that waits on a government queue. The license, not the wafer, is the lead time.

Siting has the same shape. San Jose's council voted unanimously to pause all land-use approvals for data centers and other large energy-use projects until the standards land — the trigger was the city confirming that Google's Alviso project could reach approval before the rules did. The Planning Commission recommended the standards on Wednesday and added a second gate: commissioners, not the planning director, approve large projects. The council votes December 8, and if it passes as an urgency ordinance the standards take effect immediately; the pause on new approvals extends into the new year because of the December–January recess. Dublin wants a moratorium, joining Oakland and Hayward, so the constraint is now a regional policy stack rather than one city's decision. [The 11-18 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/18/the-headline-is-a-count-the-constraint-is-a-rate/) called the moratorium a queue with a date. The operator translation is blunt: a lease or a power contract signed against an unissued permit is a bet on a city council, and a yes on December 8 does not produce an approval until the new year. The GPU is a purchase order; the permit is a calendar.

The same week, California's DMV pulled Waymo off every state freeway after one viral incident, and Santa Clara — the city that hosts a large share of the buildout — voted to hire its first sustainability manager because it has no emissions baseline for its own climate targets. The regulatory clock for autonomous driving is incident-driven, not eval-driven; the measurement clock for the host city is a hiring problem before it is a dashboard problem. Read together with the BIS rule and the license queue, the week's message to anyone planning 2027 capacity is that the critical path runs through permits, licenses and interconnects, not silicon. What gets commoditized is the capacity announcement. What gets scarce is a signed permit, an approved license, a contract with a counterparty that cannot walk. The assumption retired is that announced capacity is capacity — [the 11-16 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/16/the-access-path-is-the-control-plane/) called the access path the control plane, and this week the control plane got a rulebook and a queue.

## The week's calls — short-term predictions

Scorecard before new markers. Last week's Argon call is moving the wrong way: Google launched Neon at $3/$15 on Wednesday and Carbon and Argon step from $2/$10 to $4/$20 on December 1, so the prediction that Argon would be cut or retired looks likely wrong, but it is not scored until December 31. The seeded-fault catch-rate call is a near-miss: OpenAI's 70%-accurate audit of 100 root-cause suggestions is an accuracy number for a diagnosis agent, not a detection rate on deliberately injected misbehavior beside a blocked-action share. The default-deny egress call is also a near-miss: Simon Willison and Prime Radiant published [external sandbox servers](https://blog.primeradiant.com/external-sandboxes-for-coding-agents/) with an optional allow-list proxy — a reference implementation you can run, not a framework default. NVIDIA's external-CEO call stays open: the board said it does not intend to reinstate Huang, Kress remains interim, and the search continues. The Lambda tranche call drew no news this week. The OpenAI round call (11-01 P4) reaches its November 30 expiry with no signed close on the record; Faber's $15 billion at a $1.25 trillion valuation is a report, not a signature.

- **PREDICTION (1/5):** by December 31, OpenAI publishes the fixed evaluation corpus, tokenizer and harness behind its perplexity curve — the dataset, the tokenizer and the eval code, so another lab can reproduce the number. If a public dataset, tokenizer and harness that reproduce the curve are public by then, this is right; if the curve remains measured on OpenAI's internal codebase with no reproducible harness through December, this is wrong.
- **PREDICTION (2/5):** by December 31, an independent cryptographer — not MiniMax — reproduces at least one of MiniMax's CryptanalysisBench results from the published proof-of-concept code and posts the result publicly: a Waterfall-256 collision, a ROLLO-I-128 key recovery, or a COLM forgery. If a named outside researcher posts a reproduction by then, this is right; if the GitHub repository remains unreproduced outside the company through December, this is wrong.
- **PREDICTION (3/5):** by January 31, at least one major agent runtime — OpenAI Agents SDK, LangChain/LangGraph, Google ADK, Vercel AI SDK or Hermes — ships a documented verification gate: a configurable step where a task cannot be marked complete until a check (test suite, verifier model or human approval) passes, and the check's result is recorded in the run log. If a changelog or docs entry names such a primitive by then, this is right; if task completion remains the agent's own claim across all five, this is wrong.
- **PREDICTION (4/5):** by January 15, at least one frontier lab — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google or Meta — publishes a benchmark or model card that pins the agent harness and its version, plus the eval configuration, beside the score. If a card or benchmark post names the harness repo/version and config by then, this is right; if every published agent score remains harness-unspecified, this is wrong.
- **PREDICTION (5/5):** by January 31, at least one state attorney general other than California files or formally opens an enforcement action against an AI developer over the conduct of its agents — a complaint, subpoena or announced investigation, not a general AI task force. If another state AG's action against a lab over agent conduct is on the record by then, this is right; if California's November suit remains the only one through January, this is wrong.

## What I'm watching

Three signals would change the calls. First, the artifact behind the perplexity proposal: if OpenAI publishes a fixed corpus and harness — or a consortium does — "common limits" becomes a real standard; if not, it dies as a chart with a press release. Second, the MiniMax reproduction: cryptanalysis is the cleanest testbed in AI because the output is executable, so the first independent writeup will tell us whether executable claims get checked faster than benchmark claims — and whether an internal model can produce a result its own author cannot defend. Third, the permit and license calendars: San Jose's December 8 vote, the first approval-time data under the BIS remote-access regime, and Google's December 1 price step-up. If consumer chip licenses clear in days and AI licenses take weeks, the delay itself is the instrument — and the same week's rate-card step-up will show whether a newly public Anthropic holds its price while a competitor gets more expensive.
