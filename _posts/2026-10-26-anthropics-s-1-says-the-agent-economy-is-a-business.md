Anthropic filed its S-1 this morning, and the agent economy now has a public P&L. The headline is an $80.7 billion net loss; the numbers that matter are a $974 million operating profit in the quarter ended June 30 and $417 billion of future minimum payments on compute and data-center agreements signed since December. The first says the engine works. The second says the bill for the next two decades is already signed. On the same morning, Meta put up to $95 billion of equity on the table and SpaceX signed a $40 billion GPU lease, pricing the next block of capacity while the first public income statement shows what the last block earned. The buildout is no longer a promise about capability; it is a set of numbers with a term sheet.

## The engine works, and the $417 billion bill is already signed

**What happened.** Anthropic's S-1 reports $16.286 billion of revenue for the six months ended June 30, 2026, against $1.163 billion a year earlier — a 1,300% increase. The annual figures are audited; the six-month figures are unaudited interim statements. Compute and infrastructure expense was $8.945 billion, 55% of revenue, down from 160% for full-year 2025; the filing's quarterly table has it falling from 251% of revenue in Q1 2025 to 48% in Q2 2026. The half-year GAAP operating loss was $693 million, but the quarterlies show the turn: a $1.667 billion operating loss in Q1 2026, then a $974 million operating profit in Q2 on $11.555 billion of revenue. The $80.748 billion net loss is mostly non-cash — $72.388 billion of it is the change in fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities and SAFEs. And 48% of first-half revenue came through cloud-service-provider marketplaces, up from 11% in 2023, 32% in 2024 and 47% in 2025. The top five customers were about 25% of revenue for the nine months ended September 30, 2026, and no single customer was above 10%. The forward book is the other half of the document: $417 billion of future minimum payments on leases and commercial agreements signed after December 31, 2025 — mostly compute and data centers, terms up to 20 years — plus an estimated $125–150 billion for capacity still being negotiated, and another $245 billion of minimum payments on agreements signed or amended after June 30. Two named commitments sit inside the filing's commitment book: at least $110 billion to Amazon between May 2026 and April 2036, non-cancelable except for Amazon's uncured breach, and up to $120.5 billion to SpaceX.AI through 2029 — about $3.1 billion a month once all capacity is delivered, expected in December 2026, with each agreement cancelable on 90 days' notice after initial non-cancelable periods ending in January and March 2027.

**Why it matters.** The $80.7 billion is a valuation scoreboard: as the enterprise value rises, so does the fair value of the notes, and the accounting loss rises with it. It is not cash leaving the business. The operating line is the one to read, and it says the agent economy's leading lab is roughly at breakeven for the half and profitable in the latest quarter. The mechanism is compute economics: revenue grew faster than a committed compute base that the S-1 calls "generally fixed in the near term." That is also the warning. The ratio is a function of delivery timing as much as efficiency — a quarter when a large block lands resets it, and the filing says margins will fluctuate "particularly in periods when substantial incremental compute is delivered to us." The forward book makes that concrete: $3.1 billion a month of SpaceX.AI compute at full delivery is about four-fifths of Q2's monthly revenue, one line inside a commitment book the S-1 itself calls "substantial fixed obligations" and says depends on "our ability to generate significant revenue growth." The filing shows $482 million of property-and-equipment purchases in the half — the compute is leased, not owned. A margin rented from a fixed cost base is not a margin that scales; the trailing 55% is a snapshot of the gap between revenue and a bill that has already been signed. The second number to watch is the 48%: nearly half the revenue arrives through someone else's marketplace, where the cloud owns the storefront, the billing and the customer relationship; marketplace sales were 58% of net accounts receivable, and the top-five disclosure measures customers, not channels. Sales, marketing and partnerships cost $3.168 billion, 19% of revenue, up from 10% a year earlier — enterprise distribution is not free either. So the public picture is a working unit-economic engine with a channel it does not own and a cost base it has already committed — the tension [this column described on 10-24](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/24/the-capacity-is-the-price/) between the rate card and the bill, now with an income statement and a term sheet attached. [Sunday's zeitgeist](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/25/sunday-zeitgeist-the-bill-became-an-allocation/) called the week's theme allocation; this is the first public price of that allocation.

## The interesting number is not 18.5% — it is $135,000

**What happened.** Import AI 478 reports that ProgramBench — 200 tasks that ask a model to rebuild a program from its compiled binary and documentation — went from every tested model at 0% resolved at its May release to Claude Opus 5.5 (xhigh) at 18.5% resolved and 70.5% "almost" today; at release the best model, Opus 4.7, was at 0% resolved and 3% almost. The same issue covers Resolution's autoformalization of Hironaka's resolution of singularities: about a week, roughly 100 billion input tokens (90% cache reads) and 300 million output tokens, an estimated $135,000 in token spend, about 600,000 lines of Lean, and an orchestrator agent working from a 112-page plan that spun up pen-and-paper, Lean and support agents.

**Why it matters.** 18.5% resolved is the capability headline: a benchmark with an all-tests-pass verifier moved off zero in five months, and 70.5% "almost" — more than 95% of a task's tests passing, but not all — says the models are close on most tasks but fail the last mile. The $135,000 is the more useful number for anyone running agents, because it prices one unit of custom formal software. Ninety percent of the input tokens were cache reads, so most of the bill was re-reading context, not reasoning. That is the cost structure of long-horizon agent work: the plan, the literature and the tool outputs are the bill, and the model's thinking is a rounding error beside them. The operator's lever is the context, not the model. And "ephemeral software" becomes a budget line — a one-off artifact, verified to the last test, at a price you can quote before you start. [August's column on the harness and the price of a task](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/08/27/the-harness-and-the-price-of-a-task/) made the argument; this is the first bill that shows the unit.

## The buildout now has a term sheet, and the suppliers are on it

**What happened.** Meta launched up to $95 billion of equity financing: $35 billion now through underwritten offerings of Class A common stock and depositary shares representing Series A mandatory convertible preferred, sold in connection with forward-sale agreements, plus up to $60 billion through an at-the-market program. Meta said Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom were interested in buying up to $5 billion of the shares. Its Q3 revenue was $64.60 billion, up 26%, with negative free cash flow and a full-year capex range of $135–145 billion. Separately, SpaceX disclosed a binding commitment for roughly $40 billion of GPU financing: an Apollo-managed entity buys Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin chips and related server hardware and leases them to SpaceX for about five to five-and-a-quarter years from each delivery, with the deal expected to close by year-end and leases starting in the first half of 2027. SpaceX's AI segment brought in $7.6 billion of its $14.6 billion Q3 revenue, and the CFO guided $35–40 billion of capex in Q4 alone.

**Why it matters.** Both deals are the same instrument in different wrappers: a claim on compute that does not exist yet, sold to someone who needs it now. Meta is selling equity because capex exceeds its free cash flow; SpaceX is leasing equipment it will not receive until 2027. The cost is not the capex headline. It is dilution per share in one case and a fixed multi-year obligation against a rapidly depreciating asset in the other — the structure puts the equipment on Apollo's books and the lease payments on SpaceX's. The circularity is the other number: the suppliers of the compute are on the buyer list. That is not a scandal; it is what a capital-intensive buildout looks like when the buyers' cash flow cannot fund it. And the same filing names the demand: Anthropic's S-1 discloses up to $120.5 billion to SpaceX.AI through 2029 for NVIDIA-powered compute. The lease, the capacity and the customer are one chain. [Friday's column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/24/the-capacity-is-the-price/) said the rate card is not the bill; the bill now has a term sheet. [Sunday's zeitgeist](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/25/sunday-zeitgeist-the-bill-became-an-allocation/) called it an allocation; the allocation now has a price of capital. Watch the lease term and the dilution, not the capex number.

## What I'm watching

Thursday's Amazon Q3 call is still the test of the capacity argument: a contracted-capacity or backlog figure would turn Jassy's "sold out" into a number. October 31 is Mistral Large 4's weights deadline, five days out; November 4 is Kimi K3.1 Pro's. A weights promise is settled only by a checkpoint with a card and a license. The S-1 adds two tests: December 2026 is when SpaceX.AI's full capacity is expected, the first test of the $3.1 billion-a-month line, and the IPO's final pricing brings the first full-year compute ratio. The half-year was 55%; a full-year figure above 60% would mean the second half re-accelerated compute spend faster than revenue. And the SpaceX lease's first tranche in the first half of 2027 starts two clocks at once — the lease payments and the depreciation of the chips they are paying for.
