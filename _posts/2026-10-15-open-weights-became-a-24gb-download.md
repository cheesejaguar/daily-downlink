Compute picked a lane today, and it was the most concrete day for the open-weights argument since this column started keeping score. Nvidia released Nemotron 3.5 Bolt — a fully-open 74B-parameter, 9B-active mixture-of-experts agent model that fits in roughly 24GB — onto Hugging Face this morning, with the $2,599 desktop machine meant to run it going on sale tomorrow. Two exits away, San Jose ran the final community meeting on a first-ever rulebook for megawatt-scale data centers, complete with a published tax formula per 99MW. The frontier's two futures stopped being rhetoric: what fits under a desk, and what needs a substation and a city permit.

## The open-weights argument just became a 24GB download — judge it by what it costs to run

**What happened.** Nvidia released the fully-open Nemotron 3.5 Bolt 74B-A9B on October 15, RTX-Spark launch morning: weights on Hugging Face under `NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Bolt-74B-A9B-24GB`, with GGUF and LiLiCorr quant variants — and, the part that matters for the open-vs-closed scoreboard, training data and recipes included alongside, not just weights. It is a 74B-parameter, 9B-active MoE with 2-bit quantization-aware training, sized for roughly 24GB, with day-one support across Ollama, LM Studio, llama.cpp, vLLM, and Unsloth. A second model, a 120B-A12B Nemotron 3.5 Super VL vision variant, landed alongside it. The RTX-Spark desktop unit it targets goes on sale Thursday, October 16, at $2,599 entry, and the 64GB DGX Spark follows at $4,999 on October 23.

**Why it matters.** Separate the 74B from the 9B. The tag is dense-model branding; at inference only 9B parameters are active, so Bolt's decode cost is a 9B model's, and the entire point of the MoE shape is that a $2,600 box or a 24GB laptop can serve it. That is the reference implementation [Tuesday's scoreboard](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/13/the-frontier-got-a-uniform-list-price/) said the open side was missing: not another promise, a weight you can hold. But the trustworthy part is precisely where the independent numbers are absent. No third-party benchmark has been published yet, and 2-bit weight precision is exactly where the difference between a leaderboard number and a long agentic session shows up. The operator move is not to buy the 74B headline; it is to measure the 9B-active cost on a real workload, on a real box, against what [the bill](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/11/sunday-zeitgeist-the-bill-moved-off-the-rate-card/) now charges per seat. Fully-open data and recipes make that check possible — you reproduce, rather than take the vendor's word — which is the actual content of "open weights close the loop for operators."

## San Jose is writing the rulebook for where AI compute gets to live — city hall is now part of the supply chain

**What happened.** San Jose holds the final large-format community meeting on its proposed Data Center and Large Energy Project Uniform Standards tonight, October 15, 6–8pm at City Hall. The draft standards cover energy sourcing and consumption, water (recycled and closed-loop systems to cut reliance on potable supply), backup-generator air quality, noise, greenhouse gas emissions, design, and community-benefit terms including local hiring and labor standards, plus a defined community-notification process. Staff published the tax math alongside: a 99MW data center works out to roughly $5.6 million a year in utility-user-tax revenue at current assumptions, with large-industrial power around $0.23/kWh. The review pipeline already carries three-story, ~99MW, roughly 500,000-square-foot projects. The standards go to the Planning Commission on November 18 and to the City Council around December 8.

**Why it matters.** The realistic long pole on a Bay Area megawatt build is no longer the GPU or the model card — it is the municipal gantlet: power, water, backup-generator emissions, design review, community benefits, and a tax keyed to megawatts. San Jose is not hostile; it is pricing the buildout and setting terms, and it published the clock (November 18, December 8) because the process is the deliverable. For anyone running local models, yesterday's point [holds in sharper form](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/14/the-agent-era-got-a-steering-wheel-and-a-signing-ceremony/): the class of compute that never pulls 99MW — the 24GB box Bolt just served — clears this gantlet by never entering it. The industry is splitting into what fits under a desk and what needs a substation, and today both halves graduated on their own terms.
