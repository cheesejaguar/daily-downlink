Three numbers landed this week, each without the artifact that would let someone else check it. OpenAI published its pretraining loss curve and proposed common safety limits on perplexity, the metric it wants the industry to share — but the curve is measured on OpenAI's own evaluation set and internal codebase. Microsoft patched 3,554 security flaws in the two and a half months before its November 12 outage, and the postmortem says the outage was a July bug its lab hardware could not reproduce. MiniMax's internal model found practical breaks in historical NIST candidates; the company published the proof-of-concept code but not the model, and no outside cryptographer has run the attacks yet. The [09-20 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/09/20/the-19k-refactor-a-defense-and-a-countdown/) put the rule plainly — a number only counts when somebody can check it — and [yesterday's column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/21/stop-the-clock-after-the-check/) argued the only honest clock stops after the check. These three stories are what happens when the check is the artifact nobody shipped.

## A shared metric is only shared if the corpus is

**What happened.** OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published "Perplexity and Intelligence," an argument that perplexity — or, equivalently, cross-entropy loss — should be the metric model developers compare across labs, and the basis for shared safety standards. He published OpenAI's internal curve: log-perplexity of about 0.99 at GPT-3.5, about 0.91 at GPT-4, below 0.8 now, and about 0.76 when the model began producing what he calls groundbreaking results in mathematics. He expects further capability jumps in the 0.68–0.7 log-perplexity window and floats 2027 for a similar avalanche in pretraining research. He proposes "common limits" — thresholds on perplexity and other measurements that developers commit not to cross until monitoring reaches a concrete milestone, such as a successor to chain-of-thought monitors. The post names its own comparison problem: different labs use different evaluation datasets and train on different data mixtures, and OpenAI's numbers are measured on its own main evaluation set; a footnote says the perplexity it reports is on OpenAI's internal codebase. It also concedes that perplexity does not capture chain-of-thought gains and that nobody knows the metric's floor.

**Why it matters.** The instinct is right: a metric with a fixed definition beats a leaderboard with a moving one, and a number every lab can compute is a better basis for a limit than a vibe. But a metric is only shared if the corpus and the harness are shared. A log-perplexity of 0.91 on OpenAI's internal codebase is not reproducible by another lab, so a "common limit" on that number would be a private number in public clothing — each lab reporting its own unverifiable figure, nobody able to check the comparison. The post's caveats name the gap: cross-lab datasets differ, and perplexity measures the pretraining substrate, not the post-trained capabilities a safety limit is meant to pace; by Pachocki's own admission, chain-of-thought reasoning raises capability without lowering perplexity. The artifact that would make the proposal real is a fixed public evaluation corpus, a pinned tokenizer and a published harness — then the curve, then the limit. Publish the yardstick before the limit, or the limit is a promise. For an operator the useful part is the direction: track the substrate, but do not mistake a leading indicator for a boundary. The capability that worries you is elicited after pretraining and needs its own eval.

## The patch count is an input; the postmortem is the output

**What happened.** NBC's Kevin Collier reported that Microsoft patched 3,554 security flaws in the roughly two and a half months before its November 12 Microsoft 365 outage — more than it patched in 2025 and 2024 combined — and that the surge is largely due to AI's ability to find flaws in code. Federal officials and business leaders tell NBC that the same capability has contributed to numerous high-profile outages in recent weeks. Microsoft's own preliminary post-incident report gives the November 12 outage a specific root cause: a latent bug introduced in July in the networking gateway servers, triggered when a Teams failover shifted traffic onto that infrastructure while about 14% of gateway capacity was offline. More than 80% of the gateway servers became unresponsive; the outage ran from 11:20 a.m. ET on Thursday, November 12, to 11:10 a.m. ET the next day. Microsoft said its lab load tests missed the bug because the lab used weaker hardware with fewer cores, while the contention issue worsens on newer machines with more cores.

**Why it matters.** The two numbers describe different systems. 3,554 is a discovery metric; the outage is a change-management and test-fidelity metric. The postmortem is the artifact that says what to fix: a test rig that does not match the production SKU's core count and traffic mix is not a test of the production system, and a failover path has to be load-tested as a path, not assumed. The patch wave multiplies the number of changes that need a production-fidelity test; this outage shows the lab was not production-fidelity for a single July update. If AI keeps finding flaws faster than the lab can reproduce production conditions, the constraint moves to the rig and the rollout, not the finder. And the causal claim in the NBC piece — that the AI patch wave is making outages more common — is not established by the one incident we can inspect: Microsoft attributes this outage to a July update, not the recent patch surge. The operator's output metric is incident rate and time-to-restore, with every incident attributed from its postmortem. Patch volume is an input, and an input that jumps to more than two years' worth in ten weeks is a warning about the change pipeline, not a score. [The 10-21 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/21/the-model-is-the-easy-part/) made the patch-latency argument at internet scale; this is the same clock inside one vendor's lab.

## A practical break is a script someone else can run

**What happened.** MiniMax's research team published results from an internal-only model it calls MIM, run against Tier 2 of CryptanalysisBench — a NeurIPS 2026 benchmark from ETH Zurich, Anthropic and collaborators covering 135 publicly reviewed schemes with no known practical attacks, plus seven production-strength challenge schemes. The company reports the first practical collision on Waterfall-256, a NIST SHA-3 candidate, in 0.045 seconds on a single core; full-round collisions on Blender-256 in 0.36 to 2.3 seconds; shared-secret recovery on LOCKER in 2 seconds after shrinking the solver matrix from 42 petabytes to 58 megabytes; and key recovery on ROLLO-I-128 in about 6 seconds. It also reports a signature forgery against DRS that exploits a logical-AND written as a bitwise-AND in the reference code, with zero signing queries, in 0.1 to 0.8 seconds; a forgery against COLM, a CAESAR authenticated-encryption winner, in two encryption queries; 94 solved TII McEliece instances including 55 previously unsolved, at 6,180x to 43,400x speedups; and HAWK bit-security estimates lowered by another 9.3 to 23.8 bits, with the key recovered in 30 minutes on one core. MiniMax says the work covers historical competition candidates and academic designs, not deployed standards, and that it shared findings with the schemes' authors; it published reasoning traces and proof-of-concept code at github.com/MiniMax-AI/Research. The results are company-reported, with no independent reproduction yet.

**Why it matters.** Cryptanalysis is a clean testbed because the output is executable and binary: the script recovers the secret or it does not, so anyone with the proof-of-concept can check the claim. That is the right shape for an AI capability claim, and it is why this story is more auditable than a benchmark score. But the model is internal-only: what is reproducible is the attack script, not the model that wrote it. Independent cryptographers can verify the result without verifying the capability, which is the distinction the headline blurs. The scope is part of the claim — these are historical NIST candidates and academic designs, not deployed standards; a collision in a SHA-3 competition candidate is a result about that design, not about the cryptography under your TLS. MiniMax's own conclusion is the operator's: the bottleneck shifts from discovering attacks to verifying and assessing them, and security margins still need human cryptographers. For standards bodies, years of review and a security proof are filters, not guarantees; a design can still have a structural flaw. The practical read is that discovery is getting cheap on both sides of security, and the scarce resource is the review that can tell a break from a bug in the attack code.

## The Rest

- **Muru** — Graham Dumpleton, who started mod_wsgi twenty years ago, is building Muru, an AI-assisted Rust server for Python WSGI and ASGI applications, and documenting it in public: eighteen detailed posts in three weeks on import-once-fork-many, releases without restarts, canaries without a service mesh, scaling from worker reports and tracing in the toolbar. The useful artifact is the decision log, not the code; his line on AI is the one to keep: "The AI may be doing a lot of the work on the surface, but deciding what isn't good enough yet is still down to me." A server is a pile of judgment calls about failure modes, and twenty years of getting them wrong is the qualification. [simonwillison.net](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/21/muru/)
- **Palo Alto Networks is in talks to buy Terra Security** — the Israeli startup's AI agents simulate attacker techniques and continuously test corporate defenses, and the deal could exceed $500 million, Calcalist reports. Talks, not a signed deal. The product is verification as a subscription: agents run the attack paths so human experts can watch the results. The number the deal does not answer is the false-positive rate — an agent that finds an exploitable path every day is either a good tester or a noisy one, and the price depends on which. [calcalistech.com](https://www.calcalistech.com/ctechnews/article/4nq9tjogn)
- **Santa Clara has climate targets and no baseline** — 40% emissions cut by 2030, 80% by 2035, carbon-neutral by 2045, and no idea where the city stands; the council voted unanimously on November 10 to hire a full-time sustainability manager to build the inventory. Councilmember Suds Jain has pushed the position for 15 years. The city that hosts a large share of the AI buildout cannot compute its own emissions baseline; measurement is a hiring problem before it is a dashboard problem. [sfgate.com](https://www.sfgate.com/news/bayarea/article/san-jose-spotlight-santa-clara-to-hire-22543300.php)
- **Tesla began rolling out FSD v15** with release notes that claim a 25x improvement in the critical intervention rate from a vision encoder trained on millions of internet videos, a new "Reality View" visualization, and fixes aimed at signs, potholes and school zones — the areas the system has struggled with. The 25x is Tesla's own number, with no published baseline or methodology; an independent September test by Brussels nonprofit Johanna.be found FSD exceeded the limit in 55% of the 30 km/h zones it drove through, averaging 44 km/h. The artifact that would settle it is interventions per million miles across the fleet, with the intervention definition attached, not a render. [electrek.co](https://electrek.co/2026/11/21/tesla-fsd-v15-rollout-25x-safety-reality-view/)
- **Anthropic's IPO process was compartmented like a secret** — Reuters reports that Anthropic capped some bank deal teams at four senior bankers, used a file platform that logs who viewed what, and asked some bank officers to accept six-month bars on advising AI rivals; the listing generated more than $500 million in fees across 22 underwriters. The leak surface is a design problem: if the deal team is the perimeter, then staffing, file access and post-deal employment are the controls. A bank that takes the fee and works around the staffing rule is the failure mode. ## What I'm watching

Whether OpenAI publishes the evaluation corpus, tokenizer and harness behind its perplexity curve — that artifact, not the chart, is what would make "common limits" checkable, and until it exists the proposal is a press release with a plot. Whether an independent cryptographer runs MiniMax's proof-of-concept code and posts a reproduction; the GitHub repository is the place to look. Whether Microsoft's next postmortem names its test rig's production fidelity and its incident rate — the output number is time-to-restore, not flaws fixed. And whether Tesla's 25x claim ever gets a denominator: interventions per million miles, with the definition of an intervention attached.
