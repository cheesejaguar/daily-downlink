Three stories today measure the same thing from different sides: what it costs to be right. Mistral released the weights of its trillion-parameter Large 4 and put its API at $0.70/$2.10 per million tokens, with a Terminal-Bench score 13 points above its preview — a release-day number that is a receipt for three weeks of reinforcement learning, not a property of the checkpoint. NVIDIA published the negative result that matters more than its speedup: BF16x9 is 2.2–2.5x on a 17,280-atom system but only 1.2–1.4x on 192 atoms, and one-pass TF32 fails the force tolerance even after 1,000 steps. LangChain's OpenWiki memory layer is 5x more accurate than grep on multi-source questions, and no better — slower — on simple lookups. Capability is not the constraint. The bill for correctness is, and it comes due at load.

## The release-day score is a receipt, not a property of the weights

**What happened.** Mistral released the weights of Mistral Large 4 — Le Chonk — today: a 1.05-trillion-parameter multimodal Mixture-of-Experts with 52 billion active parameters, a 1M-token context, a 1.061 TB FP8 checkpoint across 54 shards ungated on Hugging Face, and an NVFP4 sibling for Blackwell GPUs. The weights arrived two days after the October 27 date the preview gave. The API price is $0.70 per million input tokens, $0.07 cached, $2.10 output — about 3% above the preview's $0.68/$2.09 input card. The licence is the Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight License, a modified MIT: free to use, modify, fine-tune and commercialize, with one condition — a business operating a model-as-a-service offering needs a separate commercial licence. The release-day benchmarks are Mistral's own, with Artificial Analysis's index as the independent anchor: Terminal-Bench 4.0 at 41%, up 13 points from 28% at preview with 30% fewer output tokens; Artificial Analysis's Coding Agent Index at 48%, effectively tied with GLM-5.3 while using 47% fewer tokens per task; FrontierCode 2.0 at 19.7%; CyberGym-E2E at 79%. On Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index v5 it scores 26 — the highest among Western open-weight models, against 58 for Claude Opus 5.5 and 59 for Gemini 4 Carbon.

**Why it matters.** The preview was a promise and the release is a receipt, and the gap between them is the story. Three weeks of RL moved Terminal-Bench from 28% to 41% on the same trillion-parameter architecture — which means the benchmark you plan on is a snapshot of a recipe that is still moving, not a property of the artifact. Test the weights you deploy, not the preview you were quoted. The stable commitments are the price and the licence: the price held within 3%, and the licence is now a real document — modified MIT with one commercial carve-out, the MaaS clause, the line that matters if you resell model access. The 1.061 TB checkpoint is 133 GB per GPU across eight GPUs at FP8 before any KV cache; the NVFP4 build runs on four B200/B300s. At $0.70/$2.10 the direct API is the cheap way to find out whether 26 on AA's v5 index is enough for your workload; self-hosting is a capex decision, not a default. And the workload decides the score: 48% on the coding-agent index at 47% fewer tokens is the production number, while 26 on general intelligence is the ceiling — this is a task-shaped model, strongest where the task is code or cyber, mid-table on general reasoning. [The 10-06 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/06/mistral-priced-the-open-frontier/) priced the preview and noted the licence was left for later; the later is now, and the answer is modified MIT with a MaaS carve-out. [The 10-27 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/27/the-launch-number-is-not-the-production-number/) made the distinction between a launch number and a production number; Mistral's 41% is the launch number, its 47% fewer tokens is the production number. The 2x routing arbitrage [this morning's note](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/29/le-chonk-lands-below-the-closed-mid-tier/) flagged — Mistral direct at $0.70/$2.10 against OpenRouter's $1.36/$4.18 for the same model ID — had closed by the time this column checked; OpenRouter now lists the direct card. A price you can plan on is the vendor's own; the aggregator's number is a cache.

## Reduced precision is only free if the physics survives

**What happened.** NVIDIA's developer blog published an October 29 post on BF16x9 Tensor Core math for machine-learning interatomic potentials. On a 26M-parameter PET-MAD checkpoint, BF16x9 on Blackwell accelerated the large GEMMs in the model by 2.2–2.5x for a 17,280-atom supercell, but only 1.2–1.4x for a 192-atom water box; the error stayed below the model's own non-determinism. On Ampere, TF32x3 accelerated major GEMMs by 1.7–1.9x and the full molecular-dynamics step by up to 1.5x while keeping force error near the FP32 baseline. The negative result is the sharper one: in a 539-atom Cantor-alloy relaxation, FP32 converged in 218 steps and TF32x3 in 218–222, but one-pass TF32 failed the 1.0 × 10⁻³ eV/Å force tolerance within the 500-step limit, and still failed after the limit was doubled to 1,000 steps, with forces plateauing around 1.2 × 10⁻³ eV/Å.

**Why it matters.** The speedup is a function of the workload, not the flag, and the small-system number is the one most simulations actually run. A 2.2–2.5x gain on a 17,280-atom supercell is a real result for a large batch; a 1.2–1.4x gain on a 192-atom water box is the number a working group will see on a desktop-sized problem, where the overhead of the emulation path eats the gain. The more important result is the failure mode: one-pass TF32 is 2x per step and never reaches the force tolerance, while TF32x3 holds FP32's convergence at 1.5x per step. For Physical AI, the invariant is the product. Assert energy conservation and force tolerance on the workload you ship, not throughput on the workload you benchmark; a faster step that drifts the physics is not a faster simulation, it is a different one. [The 10-27 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/27/the-launch-number-is-not-the-production-number/) made the same distinction for serving numbers; this is the test for a simulation: BF16x9's error is below the model's own noise, TF32x3's error is at the FP32 baseline, and one-pass TF32's error is the one that changes the answer.

## Compiled memory pays only when the question spans sources

**What happened.** LangChain published an October 28 post on OpenWiki, its open-source wiki compiler, used as an agent memory layer. On questions that require connecting evidence across multiple sources, an agent using OpenWiki was about 5x more accurate than one using grep tools alone. Adding an evidence graph — citations and document references turned into explicit edges the agent can follow — improved accuracy by a further 27%, doubled citation consistency, cut cost per correct answer by 58%, and reduced median answer time by 40%. The counter-case is in the same post: for simple lookups, the agent without memory was just as accurate and faster. The numbers are LangChain's own, with no confidence intervals.

**Why it matters.** The 5x is conditional on the question mix, and the condition is the whole decision. Compiling a wiki costs tokens and maintenance; an evidence graph costs edges and upkeep. For repeated cross-source reasoning — policies that amend one another, manuals that supersede a prior version, tickets that reference code — the compiled layer pays because retrieval alone has to reconstruct the relationships on every query. For single-document lookups, it is overhead: the retrieval layer adds latency and cost without adding accuracy. Measure the question mix before paying for the index; if most of your traffic is "find the line in this file," a grep tool and a good file layout is the cheaper system. The evidence graph is the part worth stealing even if you never compile a wiki: an answer that cites the same evidence twice is an answer you can audit, and a citation edge is the difference between a memory layer that sounds consistent and one that can show its work. [The 10-27 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/27/the-launch-number-is-not-the-production-number/) made the point that a launch benchmark is a workload-specific ceiling; LangChain's 5x has the same shape — the multiplier belongs to the multi-source workload, and the simple-lookup case is the production number that decides whether to build it.
