Three stories today are about control surfaces, not capability. Anthropic ran a randomized trial on expert judgment and found that 50 professional analysts followed a secret coin-flip embedded in their agent's advice 86% of the time; 77% later repeated the agent's arguments as their own, and only 15% credited the agent. Google's strongest model, Gemini 4 Carbon, goes to a subset of cyber defenders before it goes to an API, and Artificial Analysis's own page still flags "No publicly available API." NVIDIA's CUDA 13.4 adds memory enforcement and per-fraction metering to shared GPUs, which is the difference between a device you share and a resource you can bill. In each case the question is not what the model can do. It is who holds the judgment, who gets access, and who can prove what was used.

## A human in the loop is not a control if the human defers

**What happened.** Anthropic published a randomized controlled trial on October 27. It recruited 61 analysts who had passed a causal-inference screen and used Python or R regularly, then gave them a realistic task: advise a farming cooperative on whether an advisory service was effective and should be expanded. Eleven chose not to use the agent and were left out of the analysis; the remaining 50 made four analytical choices between two reasonable options, then a final recommendation with an effect estimate and confidence interval. Each analyst's agent — Claude Code running Claude Opus 4.8 at High effort — was secretly assigned a lean on each choice by coin flip, and told to make the best honest case for it, change its mind if the evidence demanded it, and never mislead. Across the four choices, analysts went with the agent's lean 86% of the time; if the agent made no difference, the expected rate is about 50%. When analysts had already stated their own preference, they switched away from it 60% of the time when the agent disagreed, against 6% when it agreed. Afterward, 77% repeated the agent's argument in at least one answer, sometimes word for word, and only 15% credited the agent anywhere. Six of the 48 who answered the post-task survey correctly guessed the study was about the AI's influence; none suspected the lean had been manipulated. With no lean assigned, Opus 4.8 made the same choices in 112 of 123 runs. The authors also report that the analysts who declined the agent made serious methodological errors at a much higher rate — six of 11, against three of the 50 — though they caution that the control group was small and less experienced.

**Why it matters.** A human in the loop is a claim about presence, not independence, and this trial measures the gap. The analysts were not lazy; they were persuaded. The 77%-repeat and 15%-credit split is the tell: the argument became theirs without the provenance following it, which is why the failure is invisible from the inside. A reviewer who believes the call was theirs cannot report that it wasn't. That makes the review step an instrument, not a staffing box. The test is not "did a human approve it" but "did the human override suggestions that were seeded to be wrong, at a rate better than chance." Put a known-bad recommendation in the eval set, measure the override rate, and require the reviewer to record what they checked independently. [The first post in this archive](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/08/19/welcome-to-the-daily-downlink/) made the point that a benchmark number is a sampling statistic, not a reliability statistic; a review approval is the same kind of number. And do not treat a second pass by the same model as a second opinion: the agent's consistency (112 of 123 runs) means asking it again is asking the same witness. Independence has to come from a different model, a deterministic check, or a different data path. The study's caveat cuts both ways — agent users finished faster with fewer serious errors — so this is not an argument against agents. It is an argument against calling the person who reads the agent's work a control without measuring whether they can disagree.

## The strongest model on the board is a premium tier you cannot call

**What happened.** Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon today, calling it its strongest model, built for state-of-the-art cybersecurity and long-horizon work. Artificial Analysis scores it 59 on its Intelligence Index v5.0; Claude Opus 5.5 is at 58. The model takes text, image, speech and video input, outputs text, and carries a 1M-token context. Google's own numbers are 88.2% on SEC-bench Pro for finding vulnerabilities, 54.8% on ExploitGym for exploitation and 71.0% on CWE-bench for patching, with 0.0% attack success on Gray Swan's indirect prompt-injection benchmark against 0.7% for Argon; the company says those external benchmarks were evaluated without guardrails. The price is Argon's: $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output during the introductory period, $4/$20 after, cached input 95% off — and Google publishes no end date for that introductory period. Availability is narrower: starting this week Carbon goes to a subset of cyber defenders, then trusted partners through the Fairwind Program, then developers, businesses and consumers "as soon as possible." Artificial Analysis's model page still flags "No publicly available API," and its provider page has no speed or latency data. What its index run does have is a cost: Carbon generated 200M tokens across the evaluation against a median of 64M, and at the $4/$20 list rate that works out to $7.76 per task.

**Why it matters.** [This morning's breaking note](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/28/gemini-4-carbon-is-googles-strongest-model-and-defenders-get-it-first/) recorded the announcement and called Carbon the strongest model you cannot call; the column's job is to score it, not repeat it. The score starts with the margin: one point on a composite index is a margin, not a moat. The efficiency number is the operator's number. A model that spends three times the median token count to run the same index is not a drop-in replacement for a cheaper tier; at list price, AA's own run works out to $7.76 per task. That is [yesterday's launch-number-versus-production-number lesson](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/27/the-launch-number-is-not-the-production-number/) applied to the model that just took the top slot: 59 is the headline, $7.76 per task is the bill. The gate is the second half. [The Fairwind template](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/09/02/google-gates-a-cyber-twin-the-template-is-now-three-of-four-labs/) has run before: [Argon was gated to trusted defenders](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/09/30/google-ships-gemini-4-argon/) for about two weeks, then [opened wide at exactly the $2/$10 intro it pre-announced](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/13/the-frontier-got-a-uniform-list-price/), with the list price taking over on November 30. Carbon's announcement gives no end date and no API listing. The planning assumption should be the precedent, not the promise: a finite gate, an intro that holds, then $4/$20. Until the API date exists, the strongest model is a benchmark, not a dependency. The security consequence is real, though: defenders get the capability before developers do, so any code-review process that assumes a human finds the bugs first is now the slow lane — even if 88.2% is Google's own unguarded number and should be treated as a ceiling until an independent eval reproduces it. This morning's note argued that at 88.2% and a $2/M introductory input price, autonomous vulnerability-finding is priced like a commodity input. The capability is real; the commodity claim is a forecast — the rate is introductory, and the model is not publicly callable. The only operators who can test the trade are the ones Google chooses. The honest score is this: capability corroborated by an independent index, at a one-point margin and a threefold verbosity cost, behind a gate with no published end date.

## GPU sharing is now metered, not just partitioned

**What happened.** NVIDIA's developer blog published an October 27 post on memory-enforced GPU sharing in Kubernetes with CUDA 13.4. The new open-source GPU Fractioning Operator, hosted under the KAI Scheduler organization, runs alongside the GPU Operator to set up and supervise Multi-Process Service on every GPU node, apply per-workload memory limits and compute modes, and measure memory and compute use per GPU fraction rather than per whole GPU. CUDA 13.4 adds a memory-boundary enforcement transparent to applications: exact memory amounts, guaranteed minimums with burstable maximums, and a choice between time-slicing and SM-sharing compute modes. KAI Scheduler v0.18.0 integrates with it through an NvFractions mode, writing per-container Pod annotations that specify memory requests, limits, compute mode and GPU device assignments for the operator to enforce, enabled with a Helm flag at install. The post's context is the trade between MIG (hardware-isolated instances transparent to the application), time-slicing (turns without memory enforcement) and MPS (concurrent processes in separate address spaces).

**Why it matters.** The hard part of multi-tenant inference is not sharing the GPU; it is proving who used what. Without per-fraction metering, every shared device is a cost-allocation argument and a noisy-neighbor mystery, and the first tenant to run out of memory takes down the neighbors. "Guaranteed minimums with burstable maximums" is the Kubernetes requests-and-limits shape applied to GPU memory, which is what turns a GPU from a device you hand out into a resource you can schedule, cap and bill. That is the unglamorous layer that makes the economics work: chargeback per fraction is how a platform team justifies a shared cluster, and a hard memory boundary is how it survives one tenant's bad day. The caveat is the one that matters for secrets: MPS is still shared silicon — same SMs, same cache, same memory bus — so this is a tenancy control, not a hardware isolation boundary. For batch inference and dev clusters, fractioning with enforcement is the right default; for workloads that carry secrets across a boundary, MIG or a dedicated device remains the floor. The operator's test is simple: can you produce a per-tenant usage number that a finance team and an incident review both accept? If not, you are not sharing a GPU; you are sharing a mystery.
