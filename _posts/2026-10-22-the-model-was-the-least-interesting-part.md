Yesterday [this column argued](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/21/the-model-is-the-easy-part/) that the model is the easy part. Today's news did not argue back. Anthropic opened a waitlist for Claude Duet, a proactive agent that decides when to interrupt you and asks before it acts; Flapping Airplanes, a year-old lab, raised $1B on the claim that a better kernel — not more data — is the next training win; and a Portuguese essay grader measured a 77% inference-cost cut by rebuilding its tokenizer. Three different layers — the interface, the training loop, the vocabulary — and in none of them was the model the thing that changed.

## Claude Duet is a scheduler with a permission hook

**What happened.** Anthropic opened a research preview of Claude Duet yesterday: a standalone iOS and Android app plus claude.ai, with a waitlist open to US Pro and Max subscribers and included in their plans at the same usage limits. It runs on Claude Opus 5.5, sends a short brief each morning on the most important items across your connected apps, then checks those apps every few hours and messages you only when something needs attention. It works on a virtual machine with its own browser, so tasks keep running after you close the app, and it can use the 6,000-plus connectors and plugins in the Claude Marketplace — QuickBooks, Stripe, Gusto payroll, Docusign, HubSpot, Shopify. Before it sends a message, makes a payment, signs a document or deletes anything, it shows you exactly what it is about to do and waits for approval; trading securities and moving money between accounts are off-limits even with approval. A new classifier and safety prompt are designed to reject the strictest tier of information, including card numbers and government IDs.

**Why it matters.** The interesting engineering here is not Opus 5.5's judgment; it is the loop around it. Anthropic's own post says Duet checks connected accounts every few hours "because checking nonstop would use up your plan's limits much faster." That is a billing decision wearing a product feature's clothes: the cadence of an always-on agent is set by the meter, not by the task. The approval gate is the other half — a commit hook with a human on the other end. Which means the throughput ceiling is your review rate, and the failure mode is approval fatigue: the day you click "approve" without reading is the day the safety model becomes theater. The off-limits list is the tell that Anthropic knows exactly which actions cannot be delegated. If you build one of these inside your own stack, the four pieces that matter are the scheduler, the credential broker, the action classifier and the approval queue. The model is the least interesting part of the product.

## A billion dollars says the next training win is a kernel, not a corpus

**What happened.** Flapping Airplanes, a year-old lab founded by Aidan Smith and brothers Asher and Ben Spector, announced a $1B Series A co-led by Index and Lux at a reported $6B valuation — nine months after a $180M seed from GV, Sequoia and Index at a $1.5B valuation. Sequoia, GV, Kleiner Perkins, Valor, Atreides, A* and Nvidia are also investors. The company's public description is a framework, not a model: a "megakernel" — one GPU program bundling many operations into a single launch — that its founders say can perform entire training runs, serve inference, play video games, or all three at once, overlapping memory-bound inference with compute-bound training so the chip rarely sits idle. The goal, in Asher Spector's words, is systems that cost less to train and run: "A model that's a million times more data efficient is probably a million times easier to put into the economy."

**Why it matters.** This round is not a bet on a benchmark; it is a bet on utilization. The megakernel claim is a systems claim, and it is the right one to make: in decode, the marginal cost of a token is set by how long the GPU waits for memory, not by how many FLOPs the model spends, and a training run that stalls on memory or leaves SMs idle is paying the same tax. If you can co-schedule memory-bound decode and compute-bound training on the same chip, you turn waiting into throughput — which is the operator's problem in one sentence. But the public record of the company is a framework and a founding team: no shipped model, no published benchmark, no named customer. A year-old lab is carrying a reported $6B valuation on a kernel that, in public, only its founders have described. The thing to watch is not the next round; it is the first artifact: a kernel release, a reproducible throughput number on a known workload, or a model trained with it. Until one of those lands, this is a thesis with a term sheet — and the valuation quadrupled in nine months on a claim.

## The cheapest inference lever is set before training, in the vocabulary

**What happened.** Dharma-AI published a preview of its VocabX paper on Hugging Face yesterday. The test case is a production Portuguese essay grader built on Qwen3-4B: same base model, same data, same training recipe, but a vocabulary rebuilt around the cost of each token rather than its frequency. The measured inference cost fell 77%; the output layer dropped from about 152,000 rows to about 65,000. The mechanics are two kinds of waste. Fragmentation: every response repeats the same JSON envelope and competency names, and a general-purpose tokenizer splits them into several tokens where one would do. Excess: the original vocabulary carried 151,669 entries, and the model scored every one at every generated token; in a 4B model that LM-head matrix was almost 10% of the parameters. Decode is memory-bound, so every unused row is data the GPU moves at every step. In their measurement, a generated token cost 7.4 times a token read. The paper is still under review and the numbers may change before publication.

**Why it matters.** Token count is not the bill. Prefill is compute-bound and reuses each weight across the whole input; decode is memory-bound and pays a full pass over the weights, including the LM head, for every generated token. A method that compresses input and output equally therefore misses most of the savings, because the expensive side is the one being generated. The tokenizer is a cost lever set before the first training step, and most teams specialize a model on domain data while leaving a general-purpose vocabulary in place — then wonder why the small model is still expensive at load. The caveat is the number: 77% is vendor-measured on one workload, from a paper still under review, so treat it as an upper bound and a direction, not a budget line. The reproducible part is the mechanism: measure your output-token share and the unused rows in your vocabulary before you shop for a smaller model. A vocabulary cut is the cheapest kind of win available this week — no new weights, no new hardware, no new model.
