A laptop, a changelog and a city council meeting make the same point from three directions this week. A 180-billion-parameter model now runs on a 32GB MacBook at about 14 tokens a second — a real capacity result, and still too slow, by its author's own verdict, for the coding agents he uses. In the little over three weeks agents spent converting 185,000 lines of curl's C into 340,000 lines of Rust, the actual curl project merged more than 300 commits and more than 200 bugfixes that none of the rewrites carry. And San Jose's city council voted unanimously to stop approving data centers until it writes the rules for them. Each story leads with a count — parameters, lines, projects — and each one's binding constraint is a rate: tokens per second, bugfixes per month, permits per calendar. [The 11-08 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/08/the-bottleneck-moved-below-the-model/) said the bottleneck had moved below the model; this week it moved into the memory tier, the changelog and the permit queue.

## A 180B model on a laptop is a capacity claim, not a serving claim

**What happened.** Tarek Ziadé added expert streaming to Transformers in PR #50717: experts mapped to `"disk"` skip Accelerate's offload, misses are read by eight I/O threads with one contiguous read per expert, and the next two layers' routers run early on the current hidden states to prefetch a guess. He ran Qwen3.8-Flash-Next — 48 layers of 512 experts, 10 active, 121 GB in FP8 — on a 32GB MacBook M5, with the experts quantized to 3 bits (49 GB on disk), a 12GiB device cache, and an n-gram embedding table that also has to be streamed from disk. After loading it uses about 18 GiB; a 6K prompt peaks at 25.4 GiB, which is the limit — close the browser, or macOS starts swapping. Against Strata on the same machine, the final numbers are 13.9 tokens a second decode versus 14.8, 228 tokens a second prefill versus 220, and 12.4 versus 13.7 after a 6K document. His verdict: close to Strata at about 14 tokens a second, and still too slow for how he uses coding agents.

**Why it matters.** The routed experts are the bulk of a sparse MoE's weights — 121 GB in FP8 for this checkpoint — so a 180B model on a 32GB machine is not a compression trick applied to the whole model: it is a bet that the router only needs ten experts per layer per token and the other 502 can live on the SSD. The engineering that closed the gap is the part worth reading: a slot-pool remap lets the GPU run `grouped_mm` on one preallocated cache tensor as if it were a normal expert bank, and what remains is about 10 milliseconds per token of host round-trips while the GPU waits for Python to queue the next layer — a PyTorch MPS limitation, not an SSD limit. The claim that survives is narrower than the headline: 180B fits, and at 14 tokens a second it is a single-user, latency-tolerant tier — offline extraction, batch summarization, a home assistant that can take a few seconds — not a serving tier for an interactive coding agent. Ask for peak RSS and cache hit rate at your prompt length, not the parameter count; 25.4 GiB on a 32GB box means one browser tab decides whether you have a model or a swap file. [The 10-27 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/27/the-launch-number-is-not-the-production-number/) made this distinction with a 185% synthetic gain that became 42.5% on a real test set; this is the same distinction one layer down.

## The curl rewrite is a snapshot; curl is a changelog

**What happened.** Daniel Stenberg counted what happened during the little over three weeks agents spent converting 185,000 lines of curl's C into 340,000 lines of Rust: the actual curl project merged more than 300 commits and more than 200 bugfixes, plus a few security fixes. In another month, he writes, curl did another 200 bugfixes and maybe fixed a few more vulnerabilities. None of those fixes are in the rewrites. His question is the one to carry into any AI-generated fork: will these clones keep up?

**Why it matters.** A rewrite is a copy of a codebase at a moment; a project is everything that lands after that moment. The 340,000 lines are frozen at that moment and inherit none of the 200 fixes that landed while they were being generated — nor the decades of bug reports, platform quirks and CVEs that produced those fixes. For a security-sensitive library, a fork that does not merge upstream is a liability with a build script, not a replacement. The operator's test for any AI-generated rewrite is not "does it pass the test suite" but "who owns the next 200 bugfixes, and does this tree track upstream?" [The 11-03 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/03/the-demo-is-the-cheap-part/) made the general case, that generation is cheap and verification is the bill; maintenance is the same law on a longer clock. The honest division of labor showed up in the MoE project above: all of its code was built with AI — the Transformers PR, the package, the Metal kernels — and the human's part was choosing what to try, reading the profiles, and deciding what to keep. AI wrote the diff; the judgment stayed human. That is the only version of this that survives a changelog.

## San Jose closed the sequencing loophole with a queue

**What happened.** San Jose's City Council voted unanimously Tuesday to pause all land-use approvals for data centers and other large energy-use projects — including the 12 proposals now under review — until it finalizes the data-center standards. The standards are designated an urgency ordinance: if approved on Dec 8 they take effect immediately, and the Planning Commission takes them up today. The pause on new approvals extends into the new year because of the December–January meeting recess. The draft standards require 100% carbon-neutral electricity, prohibit certain cooling systems that use potable water, set strict limits on backup generators, impose labor standards and require community impact fees. The trigger was the city confirming that Google's Alviso project could reach approval before the standards landed.

**Why it matters.** [The 11-09 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/09/the-frontier-model-is-a-specialist-sku/) called the sequencing the policy: a project approved before the rules were final would be exempt from them, and Google's 250 MW Alviso data center could be heard as early as December. Tuesday's vote closes that path without banning data centers — every approval now waits for the rulebook. A moratorium is a queue with a date, and the date is Dec 8; the standards then decide what "approved" means: carbon-neutral power, no potable-water cooling, generator limits, impact fees. For an operator, siting risk is schedule risk. A lease or a power contract signed against an unissued permit is a bet on a city council, and the holiday recess means even a yes on Dec 8 does not produce an approval until the new year. The GPU is a purchase order; the permit is a calendar. San Jose spent months courting the AI boom and resisted a pause until a project got close to the finish line — which is the general lesson: the buildout's critical path runs through the permit desk, and the permit desk runs on a meeting calendar. [The 11-08 zeitgeist](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/08/sunday-zeitgeist-the-week-the-model-became-the-cheap-part/) recorded 40 of 41 local data-center ballot measures passing on Nov 3; the same constraint has now arrived as an urgency ordinance in the city that spent months courting it.
