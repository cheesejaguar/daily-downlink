Three artifacts today describe the same boundary: the moment output becomes something checked. The Lean and Mathlib communities certified Levent Alpöge's 17-million-line Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis about a day after he released it — but only after a small file isolated the statement and two independent tools matched it to Mathlib's, because a compile was never the claim. Kilo published a protocol for timing parallel agents that starts the clock before the first worktree and stops it after integration and final review; its own example has three agents finishing in ten minutes and the merge eating twenty more. LangChain described the knowledge factory it runs internally, where agents flag what they cannot find and a curator agent on a cadence rewrites the corpus from raw sources and traces. In all three the output is cheap and the check is the work. The only honest clock stops after the check.

## The kernel checked the proof; the statement file decided what was proved

**What happened.** On November 20, Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as a Lean library of 17 million lines. Over the following day the Lean and Mathlib communities certified the statement and the proof: the library compiles, dozens of people reproduced the same raw proof export, and the four criteria in the Xena Project's November 12 post are satisfied. The load-bearing step was a new file that isolates the statement of the Riemann Hypothesis and imports only Mathlib; two independent tools, comparator and `con-leche --compare`, confirmed that the theorem Alpöge's library proves is exactly Mathlib's `RiemannHypothesis`. The exported raw proof was then checked by Lean's official kernel, by the `con-leche` and `con-ron` checkers — which carry formal proofs, relative to set theory, that they cannot accept a false proof — and by several independent third-party checkers written in different languages, all on an inert export out of reach of hypothetical malicious code in the proof sources. Tao's November 20 roundup describes the artifact as a proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis and adds two more AI-assisted results: a human-readable proof of finite-time blowup for the unforced 3-D Euler equations, and a Navier-Stokes blowup proof whose Lean formalization he says still needs a professionally written proof.

**Why it matters.** A compile proves that the proof compiles, not that it proves the theorem in the headline. The community's certification turns on the smallest artifact in the chain: a file that pins the statement to Mathlib's vetted `RiemannHypothesis` and two tools that independently compare it. That is the division of labor [the 09-05 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/09/05/claude-formalized-fermats-last-theorem-in-lean/) described for Fermat's Last Theorem — the statement cross-verified against Mathlib's, the kernel taking it from there — now run by a community in a day on a library four million lines larger. [Yesterday's column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/20/the-machinery-around-the-model-got-its-numbers/) flagged the statement as the open question; the community answered it within a day, which is the useful part of the story. Two residuals stay open. The first is naming: the Lean post certifies "Riemann," while Tao's roundup calls the same artifact the "Generalized Riemann Hypothesis"; the statement file is what a reader has to open to know which theorem was proved, and it is the first thing to read. The second is digestion: the kernel says the artifact is correct relative to the statement; it does not say the strategy is a proof a number theorist recognizes, which Tao expects to take time. For an operator the template is portable: pin the spec, compare it independently, and run the proof through checkers the proof cannot influence. Certification is a stack of checks, and the top layer is still a human reading the statement.

## Parallel agents are a claim until the clock includes the merge

**What happened.** Kilo published a protocol, not a benchmark. Three independent tasks — an expandable FAQ, CSV export and a search shortcut — each run in its own Git worktree from the same base commit, against the same three run sequentially; same model and reasoning settings, same briefs, tool access, dependencies and test data. The clock starts before preparing the first session and stops after integration and final review; preparation, waiting, active supervision, integration and verification are recorded separately. The protocol asks for five repeats from clean workspaces, alternating which condition runs first. Patches go into a separate branch, are reviewed for scope before applying, and the combined application runs the existing suite plus each task's acceptance checks. The post's own example is arithmetic, not a result: thirty minutes sequential against twenty in parallel is a 1.5× speedup, and if the parallel run also costs fifteen extra minutes of attention, the ten-minute elapsed gain is a decision rather than a victory. Two constraints precede the measurement: the longest task sets a lower bound on the parallel stage, and agents only help where boundaries and shared interfaces can be defined before launch.

**Why it matters.** The post contains no measured results — its numbers are hypothetical — and that is the most honest thing about it. What Kilo shipped is the denominator. Parallel-agent claims usually stop the clock when the agents return, which excludes exactly the work that scales with agent count: merge, repair and review. Patches can apply cleanly and still be wrong together, so integration repairs count even when Git reports no conflict; the milestone is not "agents finished" but "the combined app passed the acceptance checks." The metric that survives is sequential total elapsed over parallel total elapsed on comparable successful runs, with the human-attention delta beside it. A 1.5× wall-clock win that consumes fifteen minutes of supervision is a trade, and attention is the scarcer currency. [The 11-14 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/14/generation-got-cheap-review-did-not/) made the general case: generation got cheap and review did not. Kilo's contribution is to put the review inside the timed window, where it can no longer be treated as overhead. The missing piece is a published distribution — several task types, five repeats, integration minutes and merge conflicts logged per run. Until someone posts one, "three agents finish sooner" is a protocol's hypothesis.

## The knowledge factory's load-bearing part is the curator's write access

**What happened.** Harrison Chase's post argues the missing input for company agents is not a better model but better knowledge: procedures and scripts (skills), definitions and policies (domain knowledge), and guidance on where data lives and how to query it (data access). LangChain runs the pattern internally for its GTM and content-studio agents, and LangSmith Engine maintains a knowledge factory per agent. The architecture has three pieces: agents get a tool to flag missing or wrong knowledge; a curator agent runs in the background on a cadence with access to the raw sources (Notion, Slack), agent traces and the flags; storage is the least important choice (skills in GitHub, a file in Notion, OKF in LangSmith Context Hub, a graph in Neo4j). The post is explicit that the agents who use the knowledge should not maintain it — they optimize their own task and the corpus becomes contradictory — and its end-to-end example is a revenue report where an agent flags a renamed BigQuery column, a finance lead corrects a header, and that night the curator updates the relevant skills from the flag and the trace.

**Why it matters.** The factory is a write path into the context every agent reads, and the curator is a standing writer with credentials to every source plus the traces. The post names no review step for the curator's diffs; it describes a background job that updates the corpus on a cadence. That is the operator question: who reviews the writer, and what is its write scope? A corpus that serving agents read and one agent rewrites is a control-plane component, and the reviewable unit is the diff, not the document — which makes the storage choice matter more than the post allows, because only some stores give you a diff history. The design instinct is right: flagging comes from the agents doing the work, curation from a dedicated writer, and the loop closes on a recurring workflow. The salience lesson is already on the record: Anthropic's memory research found a model that recorded everything wrote 413 files for one badge, while one that recorded only what the screen could not show wrote ten for four — [the 11-07 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/07/the-checkpoint-is-the-commodity/) called it a judgment problem, not a storage problem. A knowledge factory is that judgment made institutional, and its numbers are the contradiction rate, the review latency, and whether a later session can act on what it wrote. LangChain publishes none of them yet; this is a design post, and it should be read as one.

## What I'm watching

The statement file is the thing to read: Mathlib's `RiemannHypothesis` will get a number theorist's reading, and the RH-versus-GRH question will be settled by the file, not the announcement. Kilo's protocol needs a distribution — several task types, five repeats, integration minutes and merge conflicts per run — before "three agents finish sooner" becomes a claim. LangChain's curator needs a review rate: how often its diffs are wrong, how long before a human sees them, and whether the corpus shrinks or grows. And San Jose's Dec 8 council vote is the permit calendar the 11-18 column named, with that column's caveat that the pause on new approvals extends into the new year because of the December–January recess.
