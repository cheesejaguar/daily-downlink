Three announcements in the last two days, and the interesting number is never the one in the headline. Applied Digital signed an 18-year lease on 150 megawatts and priced it in billions of dollars. OpenAI and Cisco shipped a security tool whose premise is that the patch is still weeks away. The OpenClaw Foundation benchmarked thirteen models on a hundred ordinary office tasks and found that the best of them finish six. Strip the framing from each and they answer the same question an operator asks before every purchase: what am I actually buying, from whom, and for how long. The capability is the easy part now. The term, the signature and the pass rate are what you live with.

## The megawatt has two prices and Applied Digital just sold both

**What happened.** Applied Digital signed an 18-year lease for 150 MW of critical IT capacity with an unnamed tier-one, investment-grade hyperscaler: about $5.4 billion of base-term contracted revenue, or about $12.7 billion if every renewal is exercised across a 33-year total term, at higher pricing than its prior leases. Total contracted base-term revenue now exceeds $41 billion (about $100 billion with renewals) on a portfolio of more than 1.6 GW of contracted critical IT load against 2.2 GW of grid-connected peak load, with roughly 75% of that revenue attributable to investment-grade tenants. The CFO laid out the funding order explicitly — equity already on the balance sheet, then a $5 billion programmatic equity partnership with Macquarie Asset Management, then project financing "supported by the long-term lease." Project finance for Delta Forge 1 and Polaris Forge 3 is expected in the first quarter of calendar 2027, Delta Forge 2 in the second, with delivery beginning in the first half of 2028, "subject to applicable development milestones, utility arrangements and regulatory approvals." On the same tape, Crypto Briefing reported that Applied Digital will cap its exposure to its majority-owned GPU-cloud spin-off ChronoScale at the equity it has already contributed — alongside reports of ChronoScale's six-year agreement with an unnamed frontier AI lab, covering up to 350 MW across three phases at pricing reportedly starting near $19.2 million per megawatt and valued by Needham at up to $43.7 billion. ChronoScale holds a $97 million unsecured convertible note from its parent at an $8 conversion; if shareholders approve, Applied Digital's stake could reach 85.6%.

**Why it matters.** Do the arithmetic and the two halves of this story turn into the same number measured at two layers. The lease is $5.4 billion over 18 years on 150 MW: $300 million a year, about $167 per kilowatt-month, or $214 if the renewals run the full 33. Read the ChronoScale pricing as $19.2 million per megawatt-year and it works out to roughly $1,600 per kilowatt-month — the same megawatt, sold as compute rather than as a powered shell, at about ten times the rate. The unit is what makes the comparison possible at all: $19.2 million × 350 MW × six years lands near $40 billion, the order of Needham's estimate, whereas reading it as a total per-megawatt figure gives $6.7 billion and the estimate doesn't follow. So this is one company, within a day of itself, selling the least risky layer of the stack to a credit rating and the highest-multiple layer to an unnamed lab — and choosing not to fund the second. Capping exposure at sunk equity while holding an unsecured convertible keeps the upside of the compute business without carrying its capex, which means ChronoScale has to raise the money for hundreds of megawatts of GPUs on its own balance sheet. That is what the lease's "75% investment-grade" disclosure is really for. The shell business is financeable because the signature behind it is a rating; the compute business is not financeable at that price yet because the signature behind it is a name nobody has published. Three conditions sit inside the delivery sentence — development milestones, utility arrangements, regulatory approvals — and they are where an 18-year contract quietly becomes a 2028 option. But the operator's question about this lane was never the headline total. It is who is on the other side of the contract, whether they can walk, and what happens to the debt if they do. The lease is dated November 23; [yesterday's column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/23/the-check-has-to-come-from-outside/) didn't carry it, and it should have.

## A security agent earned write access to the kernel by bringing witnesses

**What happened.** OpenAI and Cisco released Shirudo — Japanese for "shield" — an open-source BSD-2 runtime that uses eBPF to block vulnerable code paths in a running Linux system without restarting it, aimed squarely at the days or weeks between a vulnerability becoming public and a patch being deployable. A guardian agent investigates the vulnerability, writes a targeted shield and validates it against the exploit and against real workloads; security teams decide what gets deployed. Each shield is cryptographically signed and verified before loading, Linux's eBPF verifier checks the program against its safety rules, kernel security controls restrict unauthorized changes, and a Trusted Platform Module keeps a hardware-rooted record of protections loaded and removed. Operators can monitor a proposed shield's effect on applications before enforcing it. In the controlled test, a shield Codex wrote — an input check added to Java's Base64 decoder — was attached to a running Elasticsearch process and rejected a Java4Shell exploit while the service kept serving. Greg Brockman's framing is the honest one: "finding a bug today doesn't mean you can patch every system tomorrow."

**Why it matters.** The load-bearing decision is the one the announcement spends least time on. A model wrote a program that runs inside a production kernel, and the reason that is acceptable isn't the model's judgment — it's four separate gates between the proposal and the running system: a signature, a verifier, kernel-level restrictions on who may modify what, and a TPM record that outlives the process that loaded it. That is the correct shape for any agent with write access to a live system, and it is the shape most agent-security products skip, because a signature and an audit log demonstrate badly next to a blocked exploit on video. The tradeoff OpenAI does name is the one that bites operationally: an exploit and a legitimate request can rely on the same Linux operation, so a shield that blocks the first can take the second offline — which is why monitor-before-enforce exists rather than straight-to-block. What the post does not give is the false-positive rate across a fleet, or the number that decides whether a shield is a tool or a habit: how long it stays loaded. A shield is a compensating control with no expiry date on it, and if an agent can write one in minutes, the pressure to ship the actual patch weakens exactly when the shield starts working. That isn't a reason to withhold the tool; it's a reason to instrument it. The TPM log is already the right instrument, because it records the load, the removal, and the interval between — which is the only honest measure of whether a shield bought time or spent it. Ask your security team for median shield lifetime before you celebrate median time-to-shield.

## The top agent finishes six tasks in ten, and the price doesn't tell you which six

**What happened.** The OpenClaw Foundation released ShellBench today, built with Parsewave, Hugging Face, Red Hat and UC Berkeley: 100 real-world tasks in deliberately messy workspaces — emails, calendar exports, spreadsheets, receipts, PDFs — run across 13 models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, DeepSeek, Moonshot and NVIDIA, on five agent harnesses including Claude Code, Codex, Hermes and OpenClaw. The best performers — GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Fable 5.2 and Gemini 4 Carbon — completed about six tasks in ten. Meta Muse Wonder 1.0, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Claude Sonnet ran nearer four in ten. GPT-6 Astra scored 62.5% at $1.01 per run; GPT-6.1 Sol scored 61.4% at 17 cents. Repeat runs moved a long way: Astra recorded 75% on one test and 49% once the same tasks were run twice more. Of the failed tasks, 51% failed on information omission — and in 87% of those cases the agent had already collected the information it then left out. The four open-source harnesses tested, among them Hermes, OpenClaw, Pi and Codex CLI, came in slightly ahead of the proprietary Claude Code harness.

**Why it matters.** The 60% ceiling will be the quoted number and it's the least actionable one in the release. The cost spread and the variance are what you can plan against. A 1.1-point accuracy gap between a model that costs a dollar a run and one that costs seventeen cents isn't a capability difference, it's a procurement decision — and at a hundred thousand runs a month it's a hundred-thousand-dollar decision, before anyone argues about which model is smarter. The repeat-run swing is the same failure [the 11-23 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/11/23/the-check-has-to-come-from-outside/) named in its benchmark section — an interval the agent has no right to — in a different costume: a single pass yields a point estimate with no interval, and this benchmark's own repeats moved 26 points. Then there's the failure mode, which is the most useful thing in the study. More than half of the misses weren't the agent failing to find something; they were the agent failing to carry forward something it had already found. That is a context-management defect, not a reasoning defect, and it is the one an operator can fix without swapping models — tighter working sets, explicit state that survives the turn, and a retrieval layer you can inspect after the fact. Which makes the harness result the one worth watching: five harnesses on the same models producing different results means the scaffolding is a variable you control, and the open-source ones being slightly ahead is a reminder that the wrapper around the model is doing real work.

## The Rest

- **Tidal will delete 29 million AI-generated tracks on 9 December** — the platform is banning fully AI-generated music, estimating about 29 million songs removed and 80,000 to 85,000 submissions rejected per day, screened by Ircam Amplify's detector plus metadata checks. Tidal's music lead [said](https://www.theverge.com/entertainment/1041362/tidal-ai-music-ban) AI music made up 43% of the tracks distributed to the platform in the last 90 days, and that 96% of AI-generated songs receive zero plays. Both numbers are the item: nearly half of new supply earns essentially no listening, and the remedy is a classifier clearing 80,000 tracks a day. That is a triage pipeline, not a curation decision, and every catalogue platform is about to need one.

- **A robot navigation policy trained in six hours on one desktop box** — the same tutorial family [the 10-23 column](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/10/23/the-boundary-is-the-product/) covered as the agent-as-planner layer now has a controller underneath it: NVIDIA and Hugging Face published a sim2real recipe that keeps the walking policy frozen and trains a separate 50 Hz avoidance controller on top, reading an 8×8 time-of-flight grid (45° field of view, 0.12–4 m, 15 Hz) and adjusting the velocity command the walker receives. Training randomizes sensor noise, delays and missing measurements, including near the edges of the grid. The tutorial config runs 16,384 environments for 1,500 iterations; scaled to 4,096 environments and 3,000 PPO updates it finishes in about six hours on a single DGX Spark. Freezing the walker is the deployment decision, not a training shortcut: you change one small policy at a defined interface instead of re-validating the gait end to end. [huggingface.co](https://huggingface.co/blog/nvidia/microduck-obstacle-avoidance)

- **Google republished an ICML 2025 result, and its two parameter claims measure different things** — DeepCrossAttention replaces the fixed residual add with learned per-token routing across all prior layers, reporting the same quality up to three times faster training with "a negligible increase in parameters" for the routing modules — and, separately, "up to 30-40%" fewer parameters needed for the same quality. Those aren't in conflict; the first is about what DCA adds, the second about what the whole model needs. The blogged curve goes from 15.14 perplexity for a baseline transformer to 14.35 with active routing. What it doesn't publish is a serving measurement, which is the number that decides whether depth-wise cross-attention is cheap in production, since attending across every prior layer means those layers' outputs stay live for the current token. [research.google](https://research.google/blog/supercharging-transformer-residual-connections/)

- **NASA is studying a hyperscale campus at Moffett Field** — the same federal airfield whose hammer-throw site closed in [this column's August note](https://blog.aaronx.co/2026/08/24/priced-flagship-routing-layer/) is now the subject of a request for a real-estate firm to evaluate data center development on Ames Research Center land, roughly 2,000 acres mostly in unincorporated Santa Clara County. A NASA spokesperson says the study is advisory and "does not commit NASA to any course of action." The county already hosts 96 operating data centers with 30 more planned and three under construction; San Jose enacted a temporary moratorium this month. The clause that matters is the boring one: Mountain View has no zoning authority over federal land. Siting decisions are migrating to parcels the local planning process can't reach. [sanjosespotlight.com](https://sanjosespotlight.com/nasa-eyes-moffett-field-in-mountain-view-for-massive-data-center/)

- **UC students got free ChatGPT and the faculty senate says nobody asked them** — Academic Senate leaders called the expanded OpenAI partnership "unwise" and said administrators didn't properly consult them. A university signing a model contract is a procurement decision that lands in classrooms, and the people who own the classrooms found out late. That the objection is on the record is the accountability mechanism working, however belatedly. [berkeleyside.org](https://www.berkeleyside.org/2026/11/23/university-of-california-chatgpt)

## What I'm watching

Whether ChronoScale names its counterparty or its funding plan for 350 MW of GPU capacity before its next disclosure — an unsecured convertible from the parent is not a capital plan. Whether anybody publishes a false-positive rate for Shirudo at fleet scale, and whether shield lifetime becomes a metric the way time-to-patch did. Whether ShellBench's full results hold up the harness finding, since a scaffold beating a model is a more useful result than another leaderboard. And the Applied Digital project finance closing in the first half of 2027, because a lease is only worth what the lenders think of the signature on it.
